## I. LATENCY REQUIREMENTS AND RUNTIME FEASIBILITY

### 1.1 What "Real-Time" Actually Means for Water Systems

The term "real-time" means radically different things depending on the application. Each application has a hard deadline — if the model doesn't finish before the deadline, the result is useless.

```
APPLICATION LATENCY REQUIREMENTS
════════════════════════════════

APPLICATION               DEADLINE        WHY THIS DEADLINE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Pump scheduling           30-60 minutes   Must compute optimal schedule
optimization              before shift    before operators implement it.
                          change          Computed every 4-8 hours.

CSO prediction &          15-30 minutes   Must warn operators and
early warning             before CSO      regulators before overflow
                          onset           begins. Lead time needed for
                                          gate adjustments.

Flood warning             30-60 minutes   Must warn public and
                          before flooding emergency services before
                                          streets flood. Time needed
                                          for evacuation/sandbagging.

Real-time control         1-5 minutes     Control loop: sense → model →
(gate/pump/valve          per control     decide → actuate. Must complete
optimization)             cycle           within one control cycle.
                                          Faster = better control.

Leak detection &          5-15 minutes    Compare modeled vs measured
localization              per update      pressure continuously.
                                          Anomaly = possible leak.
                                          Faster detection = less water lost.

Contamination             < 5 minutes     Must track contaminant front
response                  per update      in real-time. Every minute of
                                          delay = more people exposed.
                                          Fastest possible response needed.

```

### 1.2 EPANET Runtime Characteristics

```
EPANET RUNTIME BENCHMARKS
═════════════════════════

Network Size        Timestep    Simulation     Runtime
(nodes/pipes)       (hydraulic) Duration       (typical)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
100 / 120           1 hour      24 hours       < 0.1 sec
1,000 / 1,200       1 hour      24 hours       0.1-0.3 sec
5,000 / 6,000       1 hour      24 hours       0.3-1.0 sec
10,000 / 12,000     1 hour      24 hours       1-3 sec
50,000 / 60,000     1 hour      24 hours       5-15 sec
100,000 / 120,000   1 hour      24 hours       15-60 sec

With water quality (5-min WQ timestep):
10,000 / 12,000     1 hr / 5 min 24 hours      5-15 sec
50,000 / 60,000     1 hr / 5 min 24 hours      30-120 sec

RUNTIME SCALING:
  Hydraulic: approximately O(N^1.2) per timestep
  WQ: approximately O(N × T/Δt_wq) — linear with pipe segments × timesteps
  Total: dominated by WQ for large networks with fine WQ timestep

WHAT FITS IN EACH LATENCY WINDOW:
══════════════════════════════════

5-minute window (300 seconds):
  Hydraulic only: networks up to 100,000+ nodes ✓
  With WQ: networks up to 50,000 nodes ✓
  With optimization (100 iterations): networks up to 10,000 nodes ✓
  
15-minute window (900 seconds):
  Hydraulic only: any practical network ✓
  With WQ: any practical network ✓
  With optimization (1,000 iterations): networks up to 50,000 nodes ✓

1-hour window (3,600 seconds):
  With optimization (10,000 iterations): any practical network ✓
  Comprehensive DT cycle: any practical network ✓

EPANET VERDICT:
═══════════════
For virtually all real-time applications, EPANET's native runtime 
is FAST ENOUGH without surrogate models. The solver completes 
in seconds to minutes for networks of any practical size.

The bottleneck is NOT the hydraulic solver — it's:
  1. Data ingestion and preprocessing (SCADA → model inputs)
  2. Demand estimation (sparse sensors → full demand field)
  3. Optimization algorithms wrapping the solver (many iterations)
  4. Post-processing and visualization

ML surrogates for EPANET are valuable NOT because the solver is 
slow, but because optimization/uncertainty analysis requires 
THOUSANDS of solver calls, and even 1 second × 10,000 runs = 
2.8 hours (which may exceed the deadline).
```

### 1.3 SWMM5 Runtime Characteristics

```
SWMM5 RUNTIME BENCHMARKS
═════════════════════════

Network Size      Routing    Simulation  Routing     Runtime
(elements)        Method     Duration    Timestep    (typical)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
500 conduits      KW         6 hours     5 min       2-5 sec
500 conduits      DW         6 hours     10 sec      30-120 sec
2,000 conduits    KW         6 hours     5 min       5-15 sec
2,000 conduits    DW         6 hours     10 sec      2-10 min
5,000 conduits    KW         6 hours     5 min       15-45 sec
5,000 conduits    DW         6 hours     5 sec       10-45 min
10,000 conduits   KW         6 hours     5 min       30-120 sec
10,000 conduits   DW         6 hours     5 sec       30-120 min
10,000 conduits   DW         6 hours     1 sec       2-8 hours

RUNTIME SCALING:
  Kinematic Wave: O(N × T/Δt) — linear, sequential processing
  Dynamic Wave: O(N × T/Δt) — but Δt is MUCH smaller (Courant-limited)
  
  The critical difference: 
    KW Δt = 300 sec → 72 steps per 6-hour storm
    DW Δt = 5 sec → 4,320 steps per 6-hour storm
    Ratio: DW requires 60× more timesteps for the SAME network

COURANT DEPENDENCY:
  The SHORTEST pipe in the network controls the GLOBAL timestep.
  
  Network with 10,000 conduits, shortest pipe = 200 ft:
    Max Δt ≈ 200/(5+15) = 10 sec → runtime ~30 min
    
  Same network, shortest pipe = 25 ft (short lateral):
    Max Δt ≈ 25/(5+15) = 1.25 sec → runtime ~4 hours
    
  ONE short pipe can increase runtime by 8×.
  This is why SWMM5 modelers spend effort removing or 
  lengthening short conduits.

WHAT FITS IN EACH LATENCY WINDOW:
══════════════════════════════════

5-minute window (300 seconds):
  KW: networks up to 5,000 elements ✓ (but limited physics)
  DW: networks up to 500 elements ✓ (full physics)
  DW with short pipes: maybe 100-200 elements ⚠️
  
15-minute window (900 seconds):
  KW: networks up to 20,000+ elements ✓
  DW: networks up to 1,000-2,000 elements ✓
  DW with short pipes: 300-500 elements ⚠️

1-hour window (3,600 seconds):
  KW: any practical network ✓
  DW: networks up to 5,000-10,000 elements ✓
  DW with short pipes: 1,000-3,000 elements ⚠️

Optimization (1,000 iterations):
  KW, 2,000 elements: 1,000 × 10 sec = 2.8 hours ⚠️
  DW, 2,000 elements: 1,000 × 5 min = 83 hours ❌
  DW, 5,000 elements: 1,000 × 30 min = 21 DAYS ❌❌

SWMM5 VERDICT:
═════════════
For real-time applications, SWMM5's native Dynamic Wave runtime 
is TOO SLOW for all but the smallest networks.

Options:
  1. Use Kinematic Wave (fast but loses backwater/surcharge physics)
  2. Simplify the model (fewer elements, longer pipes, larger Δt)
  3. Use ML surrogate models (train offline, infer in milliseconds)
  4. Use parallel/GPU computing (emerging, not standard in EPA SWMM5)
  5. Accept longer latency (hourly updates instead of 5-minute)

ML surrogates are ESSENTIAL for SWMM5 real-time applications 
because the physics-based solver is fundamentally too slow for 
the required latency. This is the primary driver of SWMM5 
surrogate model research.
```

### 1.4 The Runtime Gap — Quantified

```
RUNTIME COMPARISON FOR EQUIVALENT SYSTEMS
══════════════════════════════════════════

Scenario: 5,000-element network, 24-hour simulation

                        EPANET          SWMM5 (DW)      Ratio
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Single run:             2 sec           20 min           600×
Optimization
  (1,000 iterations):   33 min          14 days          600×
Uncertainty analysis
  (10,000 Monte Carlo): 5.5 hrs         139 days         600×
Real-time update
  (every 5 min):        2 sec ✓         20 min ❌        —
  (per cycle)           (288 cycles     (exceeds cycle   
                         per day)        duration!)
  
THE FUNDAMENTAL ASYMMETRY:
══════════════════════════

EPANET can run its solver WITHIN the real-time control cycle.
SWMM5 CANNOT — its solver takes longer than the cycle itself.

This means:
  • EPANET digital twins can use the FULL physics-based solver 
    in real-time, supplemented by ML for optimization.
  
  • SWMM5 digital twins MUST use ML surrogates for the primary 
    hydraulic computation, with occasional full solver runs for 
    validation/recalibration.

The topology-physics-solver chain explains this:
  Trees → free surface → Saint-Venant PDEs → explicit Δt ~ seconds
  Loops → full pipe → algebraic equations → implicit Δt ~ hours

The same physical reality that makes SWMM5 more powerful 
(captures transient wave dynamics) makes it slower 
(must track those waves at fine time resolution).
```

### 1.5 Strategies to Bridge the Gap

```
STRATEGY 1: MODEL SIMPLIFICATION
═════════════════════════════════

Reduce SWMM5 model complexity for real-time use:

Original model: 5,000 conduits, Δt = 5 sec, runtime = 20 min
Simplified:
  a. Remove small laterals (< 8" diameter) → 2,000 conduits
  b. Combine short pipes (< 100 ft) → raise min Δt to 15 sec
  c. Use fewer subcatchments (aggregate) → faster hydrology
  d. Result: 2,000 conduits, Δt = 15 sec, runtime = 3 min
  
Trade-off: lose detail in small pipes, but capture main 
system behavior (trunk surcharging, major flooding locations).

STRATEGY 2: KINEMATIC WAVE FOR SCREENING
════════════════════════════════════════

Use KW for fast screening, DW only when needed:

  KW run (5,000 elements, 6 hrs): 15 sec
  Check: does any pipe approach capacity? Any surcharging?
  
  If NO: KW result is sufficient. Done.
  If YES: run DW on ONLY the affected subsystem (500 elements):
          DW runtime: 2 min. Still within 5-min window.

This hybrid approach uses KW as a fast "pre-filter" and 
DW only where the full physics are needed.

STRATEGY 3: HOT START + ROLLING WINDOW
═══════════════════════════════════════

Instead of simulating the full storm from the beginning:

  1. Save model state every 15 minutes (hot start file)
  2. When real-time update needed:
     a. Load most recent hot start
     b. Simulate ONLY the next 15-60 minutes
     c. Much shorter simulation → much faster runtime
  
  Original: simulate 6 hours from scratch = 20 min
  Rolling:  simulate 30 minutes from hot start = 2-3 min
  
  Trade-off: accumulated errors from repeated hot starts.
  Mitigation: run full simulation every few hours to recalibrate.

STRATEGY 4: ML SURROGATE (detailed in Section II)
══════════════════════════════════════════════════

Train ML model on SWMM5 offline, use for real-time inference:
  Training: 2,000 SWMM5 runs × 20 min each = 28 days (offline, once)
  Inference: < 0.01 sec per prediction (real-time, unlimited)
  
  10,000× speedup makes ANY latency window achievable.
  But accuracy depends on training data coverage.

STRATEGY 5: ENSEMBLE OF STRATEGIES
═══════════════════════════════════

In practice, real-time SWMM5 digital twins combine all strategies:

  Level 1 (every 1 min):  ML surrogate for instant prediction
  Level 2 (every 5 min):  Simplified KW model for validation
  Level 3 (every 15 min): Hot-start DW on critical subsystem
  Level 4 (every 1 hr):   Full DW model for recalibration
  Level 5 (every 24 hr):  Complete rerun for surrogate retraining
  
  Each level provides a check on the level above:
    ML predictions validated against KW results
    KW results validated against DW results
    DW results validated against full model
    Full model calibrated against monitoring data
```

## II. SURROGATE MODELS FOR OPTIMIZATION, REAL-TIME CONTROL, AND UNCERTAINTY

### 2.1 The Three Applications — Why Surrogates Are Essential

```
APPLICATION 1: OPTIMIZATION
════════════════════════════

Problem: Find the best design or operating strategy.
Method: Evaluate MANY candidate solutions and find the best.
Need: 1,000-100,000 model evaluations.

SWMM5 Examples:
  • Pipe sizing: What diameters minimize cost while preventing flooding?
    Decision variables: diameter of each pipe (discrete: 8", 12", 15", ...)
    Objective: minimize total pipe cost
    Constraint: no flooding for 10-year design storm
    Search space: 10^50 combinations for 50 pipes × 10 sizes each
    Evaluations needed: 5,000-50,000 (genetic algorithm)
    
  • LID placement: Where to put bioretention to maximize runoff reduction?
    Decision variables: LID type and area in each subcatchment
    Objective: maximize runoff volume reduction
    Constraint: budget limit, space constraints
    Evaluations needed: 10,000-100,000
    
  • Real-time gate control: What gate positions minimize CSO volume?
    Decision variables: gate opening (%) at each controlled structure
    Objective: minimize total CSO volume over event
    Constraint: no flooding at critical junctions
    Evaluations needed: 1,000-10,000 per control cycle
    Must complete within control cycle (5-15 minutes)

EPANET Examples:
  • Pump scheduling: When to run which pumps to minimize energy cost?
    Decision variables: pump on/off status each hour (binary)
    Objective: minimize daily pumping energy cost
    Constraint: tank levels within range, pressures above minimum
    Evaluations needed: 5,000-50,000
    
  • Pipe design: What diameters meet pressure requirements at min cost?
    Decision variables: pipe diameter (discrete sizes)
    Objective: minimize capital cost
    Constraint: minimum 20 psi at all nodes during max day + fire flow
    
  • Sensor placement: Where to install sensors for best leak detection?
    Decision variables: which nodes get sensors (binary)
    Objective: maximize detection probability
    Constraint: budget limit on number of sensors
    Evaluations needed: 10,000-100,000

WITHOUT SURROGATES:
  SWMM5: 10,000 evals × 20 min = 139 DAYS → impossible ❌
  EPANET: 10,000 evals × 2 sec = 5.5 hours → feasible ✓
    (but 100,000 evals × 2 sec = 56 hours → surrogates help)

WITH SURROGATES:
  SWMM5: 10,000 evals × 0.01 sec = 100 seconds → feasible ✓✓
  EPANET: 100,000 evals × 0.001 sec = 100 seconds → feasible ✓✓

APPLICATION 2: REAL-TIME CONTROL (RTC)
══════════════════════════════════════

Problem: Choose the best control action RIGHT NOW.
Method: Predict outcomes of candidate actions, choose the best.
Need: Fast prediction (seconds, not minutes).

SWMM5 RTC Examples:
  • Gate control for CSO minimization
    - Sense: current depth/flow at monitoring points
    - Predict: what happens in next 30-60 min for each gate setting?
    - Decide: which gate setting minimizes predicted CSO?
    - Actuate: send command to gate controller
    - Cycle time: 5-15 minutes
    
  • Pump control for flood prevention
    - Pre-pump storage before predicted storm
    - Maximize available wet well volume before peak arrives
    
  • Inflatable dam control for in-system storage
    - Raise dams to store water during rising limb
    - Lower dams before peak to create capacity

EPANET RTC Examples:
  • Pump speed optimization
    - Adjust VFD speed to maintain target pressure with min energy
    - Cycle time: 5-15 minutes
    
  • PRV setpoint adjustment
    - Lower PRV setpoint during low demand (reduce leakage)
    - Raise during peak demand (maintain service)
    
  • Tank level management
    - Fill tanks during off-peak electricity hours
    - Draw down during peak demand hours

SURROGATE ROLE IN RTC:
  The surrogate replaces the physics-based model in the 
  control loop's "predict" step.
  
  Without surrogate:
    Sense → [run full model: 20 min] → Decide → Actuate
    ❌ Model takes longer than control cycle
    
  With surrogate:
    Sense → [ML inference: 0.01 sec] → Decide → Actuate
    ✓ Entire cycle in seconds

APPLICATION 3: UNCERTAINTY ANALYSIS
════════════════════════════════════

Problem: How uncertain are model predictions?
Method: Vary uncertain inputs, observe output variability.
Need: 1,000-100,000 model runs (Monte Carlo or similar).

Uncertain inputs in SWMM5:
  • Rainfall: intensity, duration, temporal pattern
  • Subcatchment: imperviousness (±10%), width (±25%)
  • Infiltration: conductivity (±50%), initial deficit (±30%)
  • Conduit: roughness (±20%), cross-section (±5%)
  • LID performance: media conductivity (±40%)

Uncertain inputs in EPANET:
  • Demands: base demand (±15%), pattern shape (±10%)
  • Roughness: C-factor (±15% per group)
  • Source head: reservoir level (±2 ft)
  • Pump curve: actual vs. rated (±5%)
  • Pipe status: unknown closed valves

Uncertainty propagation:
  1. Define probability distributions for each uncertain input
  2. Sample from distributions (Latin Hypercube Sampling)
  3. Run model for each sample → get output distribution
  4. Report: P(flooding > X gallons) = Y%
     or: P(pressure < 20 psi at node Z) = Y%

WITHOUT SURROGATES:
  SWMM5: 10,000 samples × 20 min = 139 DAYS ❌
  EPANET: 10,000 samples × 2 sec = 5.5 hours ✓ (marginal)

WITH SURROGATES:
  SWMM5: 10,000 samples × 0.01 sec = 100 sec ✓✓
  EPANET: 10,000 samples × 0.001 sec = 10 sec ✓✓
```

### 2.2 Surrogate Model Training Workflow

```
THE UNIVERSAL SURROGATE WORKFLOW
════════════════════════════════

Phase 1: DESIGN OF EXPERIMENTS (Sampling)
─────────────────────────────────────────
Goal: Select input parameter combinations that efficiently 
      span the input space.

Method: Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS)
  - Divide each input parameter's range into N equal intervals
  - Ensure each interval is sampled exactly once
  - Result: N samples that uniformly cover the input space
  - Typically: N = 500-5,000 samples

For SWMM5:
  Inputs sampled:
    • Rainfall: depth (1-6 inches), duration (1-24 hrs), 
      temporal pattern (SCS Type I/IA/II/III, Huff quartiles)
    • Antecedent conditions: dry period (0-30 days)
    • Subcatchment parameters: %imperv (20-90%), width, CN
    • Conduit roughness: Manning's n (0.010-0.025)
  
  Each sample = one complete SWMM5 input file
  Run all N SWMM5 simulations (can be parallelized)

For EPANET:
  Inputs sampled:
    • Demand multipliers per zone (0.5-2.0)
    • Pipe roughness per group (60-150 H-W C)
    • Pump status combinations (on/off)
    • Tank initial levels (min to max)
    • Pipe status (open/closed — for failure scenarios)
  
  Each sample = one EPANET scenario
  Run all N EPANET simulations (very fast)

Phase 2: FEATURE ENGINEERING
────────────────────────────
Transform raw inputs into features the ML model can learn from:

For SWMM5:
  • Rainfall: total depth, peak intensity, duration, centroid
  • Antecedent: days since last rain, season indicator
  • Subcatchment: area-weighted imperviousness, average CN, 
    total impervious area, total pervious area
  • Conduit: weighted average Manning's n, min slope, 
    total pipe length, critical d/D at design flow

For EPANET:
  • Demand: total system demand, zone demand ratios, 
    demand pattern hour, peaking factor
  • Network: pump status vector, valve status vector,
    tank level vector
  • Pipe: roughness group values, known pipe closures

Phase 3: MODEL TRAINING
───────────────────────
Split data: 70% training, 15% validation, 15% test

Train model (architecture depends on topology — see Section III):
  SWMM5 → LSTM, Tree-LSTM, or 1D CNN
  EPANET → GNN, GAT, or feedforward ANN

Loss function: MSE between predicted and simulated outputs
Optimizer: Adam with learning rate scheduling
Epochs: 100-500 (with early stopping on validation loss)
Regularization: dropout (0.1-0.3), weight decay

Phase 4: VALIDATION
───────────────────
Test on held-out data (never seen during training):

Metrics:
  • R² (coefficient of determination): > 0.90 target
  • RMSE (root mean square error): context-dependent
  • NSE (Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency): > 0.85 for hydrographs
  • Peak flow error: < 15%
  • Volume error: < 10%
  • Maximum pressure error: < 3 psi

CRITICAL: Test on EXTREME scenarios (outside training range)
  The surrogate must handle the scenarios that MATTER MOST 
  (100-year storm, compound failures) — which are by definition 
  at the edges of the training distribution.

Phase 5: DEPLOYMENT
───────────────────
Integrate surrogate into operational workflow:

  For optimization: replace SWMM5/EPANET in the inner loop
  For RTC: deploy as inference server (TensorFlow Serving, ONNX)
  For uncertainty: run Monte Carlo with surrogate
  
  Periodic re-validation: 
    Every week/month, run full SWMM5/EPANET for comparison.
    If surrogate accuracy degrades (system changes, new demands,
    infrastructure modifications), RETRAIN with new data.
```

### 2.3 Surrogate Accuracy — What Can Go Wrong

```
FAILURE MODE 1: EXTRAPOLATION BEYOND TRAINING RANGE
════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Training data: storms up to 4 inches total rainfall
Real event: 6-inch storm (outside training range)

The surrogate has NEVER SEEN a 6-inch storm.
It must EXTRAPOLATE beyond its training data.
ML models are notoriously bad at extrapolation.

Consequence:
  Surrogate predicts peak flow = 45 CFS
  SWMM5 would predict peak flow = 72 CFS
  Flooding occurs that the surrogate didn't predict.
  Real-time control decisions based on surrogate are WRONG.

Mitigation:
  a. Include extreme events in training data (even if synthetic)
  b. Use physics-informed training (conservation laws constrain 
     extrapolation)
  c. Monitor surrogate confidence: if input is far from training 
     data, flag as "low confidence" and run full model instead
  d. Ensemble of surrogates: if they disagree significantly, 
     run full model for verification

FAILURE MODE 2: SYSTEM CHANGES NOT IN TRAINING DATA
════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Training data: current network configuration
Reality: new pipe installed, old pipe collapsed, 
         new development added impervious area

The surrogate learned the OLD system.
The CURRENT system is different.
The surrogate's predictions are based on a system that 
no longer exists.

Mitigation:
  a. Retrain when infrastructure changes
  b. Use transfer learning (fine-tune with small new dataset)
  c. Include infrastructure variation in training 
     (randomly close pipes, add area, change roughness)

FAILURE MODE 3: REGIME CHANGES
══════════════════════════════

Training data: normal operations (pipes partially full)
Real event: extreme storm causes widespread surcharging

Surcharging is a REGIME CHANGE — the physics fundamentally 
shifts from free-surface to pressurized flow.
If the training data doesn't include many surcharged events,
the surrogate cannot predict surcharge behavior accurately.

This is particularly problematic for SWMM5 surrogates because 
the Preissmann slot transition is highly nonlinear — small 
input changes cause large output changes near d/D = 1.0.

Mitigation:
  a. Ensure training data includes surcharged scenarios
  b. Use separate surrogates for normal and surcharged conditions
  c. Detect regime change (d/D > 0.9 at any node) and switch 
     to full model for that control cycle

FAILURE MODE 4: TEMPORAL MISALIGNMENT
═════════════════════════════════════

Training data: 5-minute resolution
Real data: arrives irregularly (sensor delays, dropouts)

The surrogate expects input at regular intervals.
Real SCADA data has gaps, delays, and noise.
Missing or delayed rainfall data causes the surrogate to 
use wrong inputs → wrong predictions.

Mitigation:
  a. Train with noisy/gapped data (data augmentation)
  b. Use architectures robust to missing data (attention mechanisms)
  c. Implement input quality checks before surrogate inference
```

## III. TOPOLOGY AND ML ARCHITECTURE MATCHING

### 3.1 The Principle: Match Information Flow

```
THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE
══════════════════════

In the physical system, information propagates in a specific pattern.
In the neural network, information should propagate the SAME way.

SWMM5 (Tree):
  Physical information flow: TEMPORAL
    Rainfall at time t → infiltration loss → runoff at t+Δt
    → routing through pipe → arrives at downstream node at t+T
    → continues downstream → exits at outfall at t+T+T'
    
  The output (flow hydrograph) is a TRANSFORMATION of the 
  input (rainfall hyetograph) through a series of TIME-DEPENDENT 
  processes (infiltration, overland flow, pipe routing).
  
  Neural architecture should process SEQUENCES:
    Input sequence → Hidden state evolution → Output sequence
    LSTM, GRU, Transformer, 1D CNN — all sequence processors

EPANET (Loop):
  Physical information flow: SPATIAL
    Change demand at node A → pressure changes at A
    → flow redistributes in pipes connected to A
    → pressure changes at nodes connected to those pipes
    → continues spreading through the network
    → entire network reaches new equilibrium SIMULTANEOUSLY
    
  The output (pressure field) is a SPATIAL DISTRIBUTION 
  determined by the NETWORK TOPOLOGY and boundary conditions.
  
  Neural architecture should process GRAPHS:
    Node features + Edge features + Graph structure → Node outputs
    GNN, GAT, GraphSAGE, ChebNet — all graph processors
```

### 3.2 Why LSTM Works for Trees but Not Loops

```
LSTM FOR SWMM5 TREES — WHY IT WORKS:
═════════════════════════════════════

The LSTM cell has three gates:
  Forget gate: decides what to forget from long-term memory
  Input gate: decides what new information to store
  Output gate: decides what to output at this timestep

These gates map naturally to SWMM5's hydrologic processes:

  Forget gate ↔ Recession/drainage
    "Forget" stored water from previous timesteps
    as soil drains, depressions empty, pipes discharge.
    
  Input gate ↔ Rainfall/infiltration
    "Store" new water from current rainfall
    modulated by infiltration capacity (soil absorbs some,
    rest becomes input to the system).
    
  Output gate ↔ Runoff/routing
    "Output" the flow to downstream based on current storage
    and current conditions (depth, gradient).

The LSTM's hidden state h(t) is analogous to the SWMM5 model's 
state vector: [all junction depths, all conduit flows, all 
subcatchment soil moisture values]. Both evolve through time 
as rainfall drives the system.

EXAMPLE — Training an LSTM for a SWMM5 Tree:
─────────────────────────────────────────────

Network: 20 subcatchments → 15 junctions → 12 conduits → 1 outfall

Input at each timestep (5-min intervals):
  [rainfall_intensity, cumulative_rain, temperature, hour_of_day]
  = 4 features per timestep

Output at each timestep:
  [flow_at_outfall, depth_at_junction_5, depth_at_junction_12]
  = 3 outputs per timestep (monitoring locations only)

Sequence length: 144 timesteps (12 hours × 12 steps/hour)

Architecture:
  Input: [144 × 4] tensor
  LSTM Layer 1: 128 units → [144 × 128] hidden states
  LSTM Layer 2: 64 units → [144 × 64] hidden states
  Dense: 64 → 32 → 3 outputs per timestep
  Total parameters: ~150,000
  
Training:
  2,000 synthetic storms → 2,000 SWMM5 simulations
  Each sim produces a [144 × 3] output time series
  Train to minimize MSE between LSTM output and SWMM5 output
  
Performance:
  NSE: 0.92 (excellent hydrograph shape reproduction)
  Peak flow error: 11% (good for 2,000 training samples)
  Volume error: 5% (excellent)
  Time to peak error: 1 timestep (5 min — very good)

LSTM FOR EPANET LOOPS — WHY IT FAILS:
════════════════════════════════════

Problem 1: EPANET's output is SPATIAL, not temporal.
  The primary output is pressure at ALL nodes at a given time.
  An LSTM processes one sequence — it would need a separate 
  LSTM for each node, with no interaction between them.
  But pressures at nodes interact SIMULTANEOUSLY through 
  the looped network — the LSTM can't capture this.

Problem 2: EPANET's input-output relationship is INSTANTANEOUS.
  Changing demand at node A instantly changes pressure at node B 
  (within the same EPANET timestep). There is no temporal lag 
  to learn. The LSTM's strength is learning temporal patterns — 
  but EPANET's hydraulic response has no temporal pattern to learn.

Problem 3: Network topology is invisible to LSTM.
  The LSTM doesn't know that nodes 3 and 7 are connected by 
  a 12" pipe while nodes 3 and 15 are not connected at all.
  The spatial structure of the network — which is EVERYTHING 
  in EPANET — is not represented in the LSTM architecture.

When LSTM IS used for EPANET, it's for DEMAND FORECASTING:
  Input: [time_of_day, day_of_week, temperature, historical_demand]
  Output: [predicted_demand_next_24_hours]
  
  This works because demand IS a time series.
  But the EPANET solver itself (pressure computation) 
  is not well-suited to LSTM surrogation.
```

### 3.3 Why GNN Works for Loops but Needs Adaptation for Trees

```
GNN FOR EPANET LOOPS — WHY IT WORKS:
═════════════════════════════════════

The GNN's message-passing mechanism IS the Gradient Algorithm:

EPANET Gradient Algorithm (one iteration):
  For each node i:
    Collect flow information from all connected pipes
    Update head estimate based on mass balance + energy equations
    Send updated information back to neighbors
  Repeat until converged.

GNN Message Passing (one layer):
  For each node i:
    Collect messages from all connected nodes (through edges)
    Update node hidden state based on aggregated messages + own features
    Send updated representation to neighbors
  Stack K layers for K-hop information propagation.

The structural analogy is EXACT:
  GNN node ↔ EPANET junction
  GNN edge ↔ EPANET pipe
  GNN message ↔ flow/headloss information
  GNN hidden state ↔ node head/pressure
  GNN layer ↔ one iteration of Newton-Raphson
  
  K GNN layers ↔ K Newton-Raphson iterations
  (Typical: K=4-8 GNN layers ↔ 5-15 NR iterations)

The GNN INHERENTLY captures:
  • Network topology (which nodes connect to which)
  • Pipe properties (encoded as edge features)
  • Demand distribution (encoded as node features)
  • Flow redistribution through loops (multi-hop message passing)

EXAMPLE — GNN for EPANET Pressure Estimation:
──────────────────────────────────────────────

Network: 500 junctions, 600 pipes, 1 reservoir, 2 tanks

Node features (per node):
  [elevation, base_demand, demand_multiplier, node_type_onehot]
  = 6 features per node

Edge features (per pipe):
  [diameter, length, roughness, minor_loss, status, type_onehot]
  = 8 features per edge

Architecture:
  Node embedding: 6 → 64 dimensions (MLP)
  Edge embedding: 8 → 64 dimensions (MLP)
  
  Message Passing Layers (×6):
    Edge update: MLP(h_source || h_target || e_features) → e_hidden
    Node update: MLP(h_node || Aggregate(e_hidden from neighbors)) → h_node
    
  Output MLP: h_node → pressure_prediction (1 value per node)
  
  Total parameters: ~500,000

Training:
  1,000 EPANET scenarios (varying demands, pump status, pipe status)
  Each scenario: 500 pressure values + 600 flow values
  
Performance:
  Pressure R²: 0.97
  Pressure RMSE: 1.2 psi
  Flow R²: 0.93
  Generalization to unseen demand patterns: R² > 0.90

GNN FOR SWMM5 TREES — ADAPTATION NEEDED:
═════════════════════════════════════════

Problem: SWMM5 outputs are TIME-VARYING, not just spatial.
  At time t=0: all depths are 0 (dry weather)
  At t=30 min: rainfall begins, depths start rising
  At t=2 hrs: peak flow, maximum depths
  At t=6 hrs: recession, depths falling
  
  A standard GNN gives a SNAPSHOT (one spatial distribution).
  We need a MOVIE (spatial distribution evolving through time).

Solution: SPATIO-TEMPORAL GNN
  Combine graph structure (GNN) with temporal processing (LSTM/GRU):
  
  Architecture options:
  
  Option A: GNN then LSTM
    1. At each timestep, run GNN on the network graph → get spatial features
    2. Feed spatial features into LSTM → capture temporal evolution
    3. LSTM output = predicted depth/flow at each node at next timestep
  
  Option B: LSTM then GNN  
    1. At each node, run LSTM on local time series → get temporal features
    2. Feed temporal features into GNN → capture spatial interactions
    3. GNN output = predicted depth/flow incorporating both time and space
  
  Option C: Interleaved GNN-LSTM
    1. At each timestep:
       a. GNN message passing → spatial interaction
       b. LSTM update → temporal evolution
    2. Repeat for each timestep in the prediction window

Additional tree-specific considerations:
  • DIRECTED edges: flow goes upstream → downstream (mostly)
    Use directed GNN (separate messages for upstream/downstream)
  • SUBCATCHMENT inputs: each node receives lateral inflow
    Include rainfall × subcatchment parameters as node features
  • REGIME CHANGE at surcharge: d/D crossing 1.0
    Include binary surcharge indicator as additional feature
    Or use separate GNN layers for normal and surcharged conditions

This spatio-temporal GNN for SWMM5 is an ACTIVE RESEARCH AREA.
The 2024 Water Research paper (from search results) on 
"GNN-based surrogate modelling for real-time hydraulic 
prediction of urban drainage networks" is one of the 
first publications demonstrating this approach.
```

### 4.2 Practitioner Experience — What the Community Says

```
FROM THE OPENSWMM FORUM DISCUSSIONS:
════════════════════════════════════

PRACTITIONER VIEW 1 (experienced consultant):
"We often use EPAnet for modeling lift station and forcemain 
systems instead of SWMM. Just need to enter flow patterns to 
represent dry weather flow as a negative demand."

WHY: EPANET's pressure output, minor loss modeling, and 
visualization are better suited for analyzing HGL profiles 
along force mains. The steady-state analysis gives clean 
operating point determination.

PRACTITIONER VIEW 2 (municipal engineer):
"For sanitary pump stations I tend towards EPAnet based programs. 
Partly because of the dent still in my forehead from SWMM4 days 
and because the reporting/visualization features of EPAnet are 
better suited for pressure systems."

WHY: Historical difficulty with force mains in older SWMM versions.
SWMM5 improved significantly (FORCE_MAIN cross-section type added),
but the reputation for being "tricky" persists.

PRACTITIONER VIEW 3 (SWMM5 expert — Lew Rossman):
"To make the SWMM5 network equivalent to the EPANET network you 
have to make all of your conduits force mains, represent the source 
node as an outfall with a fixed head equal to EPANET's reservoir 
head, set all of the node inverts to 0 and the maximum depths to 
a large value, and use negative inflows at the junctions to 
represent demands."

WHY: Demonstrates that SWMM5 CAN replicate EPANET results for 
pressurized systems, but it takes careful setup. The equivalence 
has been verified — flows and heads match to within 1-2%.

PRACTITIONER VIEW 4 (force main specialist):
"There is something fishy about the way SWMM5 handles forcemains. 
I've observed the same problem as you when I have more than one 
forcemain conduit, and especially when I am connecting the 
forcemain segments at a high point."

WHY: SWMM5's dynamic wave solver applies open-channel assumptions
(critical depth) at internal high points, which can cause force 
main segments to report d/D < 1.0 even when pressurized.

PRACTITIONER VIEW 5 (pragmatic approach):
"We prefer the relative simplicity of Epanet as a first cut model 
before jumping into the complexity of SWMM. We use Epanet to study 
the potential interaction between competing lift stations, look at 
pump run time vs. wet well sizing, investigate different pump 
options, etc."

WHY: EPANET runs in seconds, making it ideal for rapid what-if 
analysis during design. Once the design is finalized, SWMM5 can 
be used for detailed operational analysis if needed.

PRACTITIONER VIEW 6 (systems perspective):
"SWMM can model pump stations and force mains. No need for EPANET. 
If you want to model gravity systems that contain wet wells, pump 
stations and force mains, then SWMM and almost all modern sewer 
modeling software can do that."

WHY: For integrated collection system modeling where the force 
main is just one component, keeping everything in SWMM5 avoids 
the complexity of maintaining two separate models.
```

### 4.4 The Recommended Workflow for Force Main Projects

```
THE THREE-PHASE FORCE MAIN WORKFLOW
════════════════════════════════════

PHASE 1: PRELIMINARY DESIGN (EPANET)
─────────────────────────────────────
Duration: 1-2 days
Purpose: Select pipe size, pump, and verify feasibility

Steps:
  1. Build simple EPANET model:
     - Reservoir (wet well at design water level)
     - Pump (candidate pump curve)
     - Force main pipes (candidate diameters)
     - Discharge point (reservoir at receiving manhole head)
  
  2. Run steady-state scenarios:
     - Single pump, low flow (dry weather)
     - Single pump, peak flow
     - Two pumps (if duplex station), peak flow
     - Future buildout flows
  
  3. Check:
     - Pump operating point on curve (not too far left or right)
     - HGL profile (above pipe at high points, not excessive at low points)
     - Velocity (2-8 ft/s range)
     - Total dynamic head (within pump capability)
     - Available NPSH at pump (prevent cavitation)
  
  4. Iterate: try different pipe diameters, pump models
     Each iteration: < 1 second runtime
     20 iterations: 20 seconds total
  
  5. Deliverable: selected pipe size, pump model, HGL profile

PHASE 2: DETAILED DESIGN (SWMM5 — if part of collection system)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Duration: 1-2 weeks
Purpose: Verify performance under dynamic operating conditions

Steps:
  1. Build SWMM5 model of entire pump station catchment:
     - Upstream gravity sewers and subcatchments
     - Wet well with correct geometry (area vs. depth)
     - Pump with selected curve from Phase 1
     - Force main with FORCE_MAIN cross-section type
     - Pump control rules (on/off depths or rule-based)
     - Downstream gravity connection
  
  2. Run design storm simulations:
     - 2-year, 10-year, 25-year storms
     - Multiple durations (1-hr, 3-hr, 6-hr, 24-hr)
     - Antecedent wet and dry conditions
  
  3. Analyze:
     - Pump cycling frequency and duration
     - Wet well high water alarm frequency
     - Force main flow vs. time (matches pump curve?)
     - Downstream junction behavior (surcharge? flooding?)
     - Continuity error (< 1%?)
  
  4. Validate against Phase 1:
     - Steady-state operating point should match EPANET
     - If it doesn't: check FM setup (see Problem 4 in Section 1.5)
  
  5. Deliverable: pump station design package with dynamic analysis

PHASE 3: SURGE ANALYSIS (MOC TOOL — if needed)
──────────────────────────────────────────────
Duration: 1-3 days
Purpose: Verify force main can survive transient events

When needed:
  - Force main > 1,000 ft long
  - Pump > 50 HP
  - Force main has significant elevation changes (high points)
  - Community has experienced force main breaks
  - Owner requires transient analysis (increasingly standard)

Steps:
  1. Import EPANET model from Phase 1 into MOC tool
  2. Add transient parameters:
     - Pipe material properties (Young's modulus, wall thickness)
     - Pump inertia (WR² from manufacturer)
     - Check valve characteristics
  3. Run transient scenarios:
     - Pump trip (power failure) — most common scenario
     - Pump startup against empty/partial force main
     - Check valve slam
     - Multiple pump trip (all pumps simultaneously)
  4. Check:
     - Maximum pressure vs. pipe pressure rating (safety factor ≥ 1.5)
     - Minimum pressure vs. vapor pressure (-14.5 psi absolute)
     - Check valve slam force
     - Pressure at air valve locations
  5. If unacceptable: design surge protection
     - Air/vacuum valves at high points
     - Surge tank or air chamber at pump discharge
     - Flywheel on pump motor
     - Slow-closing check valve
     - VFD with controlled ramp-down
  6. Rerun MOC to verify protection adequacy
  7. Deliverable: surge analysis report, surge protection specifications
```
