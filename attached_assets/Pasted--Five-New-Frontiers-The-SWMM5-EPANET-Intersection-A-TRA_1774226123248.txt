# Five New Frontiers: The SWMM5/EPANET Intersection

---

## A. TRANSIENT ANALYSIS — THE GAP BETWEEN SWMM5 AND EPANET

### A.1 The Three Physics Regimes

There are three fundamentally different types of hydraulic behavior in pipes, and no single software handles all three:

```
REGIME 1: FREE-SURFACE FLOW (Open Channel)
══════════════════════════════════════════
Physics: Water has a free surface. Depth varies.
         Gravity waves propagate at c = √(gA/T)
         Typical wave speed: 3-20 ft/s
         
Solver:  SWMM5 Dynamic Wave
         Saint-Venant equations
         Δt = 1-30 seconds (Courant-limited)
         
Examples: Storm sewers, sanitary sewers (normal flow),
          open channels, culverts flowing partially full

REGIME 2: QUASI-STEADY PRESSURIZED FLOW
═══════════════════════════════════════
Physics: Pipe always full. Pressure varies slowly.
         Changes propagate "instantaneously" (relative to timestep)
         Steady-state equilibrium at each timestep
         
Solver:  EPANET Gradient Algorithm
         Algebraic energy + continuity equations
         Δt = 1 hour (operationally driven)
         
Examples: Water distribution normal operations,
          pump scheduling, tank filling cycles

REGIME 3: PRESSURE TRANSIENTS (Water Hammer)
════════════════════════════════════════════
Physics: Pipe always full. Pressure varies RAPIDLY.
         Acoustic pressure waves propagate at a = 2,000-4,000 ft/s
         Pressure spikes can be 10-100× normal operating pressure
         Events last milliseconds to seconds
         
Solver:  Method of Characteristics (MOC)
         1D wave equation for compressible fluid in elastic pipe
         Δt = Δx/a (typically 0.001-0.01 seconds)
         
Examples: Valve slam, pump trip, hydrant closure,
          column separation, vapor cavity collapse

THE GAP:
════════
         Free Surface ←──── SWMM5 lives here
                    ↕
         Surcharge transition ←── Preissmann slot (approximate)
                    ↕
         Steady pressurized ←── EPANET lives here
                    ↕
         Pressure transients ←── NEITHER model handles this
                    ↕
         Water hammer / surge ←── Need: HAMMER, AFT Impulse,
                                         TSNet, KYPipe Surge,
                                         Bentley HAMMER, etc.
```

### A.2 The Physics of Water Hammer

Water hammer occurs when flow in a pressurized pipe changes rapidly — creating pressure waves that travel at acoustic speed through the pipe:

```
THE JOUKOWSKY EQUATION (Maximum Pressure Rise)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

ΔP = ρ × a × ΔV

Where:
  ΔP = pressure rise (Pa or psi)
  ρ  = water density (1,000 kg/m³ or 1.94 slugs/ft³)
  a  = acoustic wave speed in the pipe (ft/s or m/s)
  ΔV = change in velocity (ft/s or m/s)

The wave speed a depends on:
  - Water bulk modulus (Kw = 300,000 psi)
  - Pipe material elasticity (E)
  - Pipe diameter (D)
  - Pipe wall thickness (e)
  
  a = √(Kw/ρ) / √(1 + (Kw × D)/(E × e))
  
  For steel pipe: a ≈ 4,000 ft/s
  For ductile iron: a ≈ 3,500 ft/s
  For PVC: a ≈ 1,200-1,500 ft/s (more elastic → slower wave)
  For concrete: a ≈ 3,000-4,000 ft/s

EXAMPLE — Rapid Valve Closure:
═══════════════════════════════

A 12" ductile iron pipe carries water at 5 ft/s.
A valve closes instantaneously.
Wave speed a = 3,500 ft/s.

ΔP = 1.94 × 3,500 × 5 = 33,950 lbf/ft² = 236 psi

Normal operating pressure: 60 psi
Pressure during water hammer: 60 + 236 = 296 psi

Pipe pressure rating: 350 psi (typical ductile iron Class 52)
→ Pipe SURVIVES, but barely. Joints, fittings, and older 
  segments may not.

If velocity were 8 ft/s:
  ΔP = 1.94 × 3,500 × 8 = 377 psi
  Total pressure = 60 + 377 = 437 psi
  → EXCEEDS pipe rating. FAILURE likely.

NEGATIVE PRESSURE (downsurge):
  When the reflected wave returns: P drops to 60 - 236 = -176 psi
  Water CANNOT sustain negative pressure below vapor pressure (~-14.5 psi)
  → COLUMN SEPARATION occurs
  → Vapor cavity forms
  → When cavity collapses: SECONDARY pressure spike
     often WORSE than the initial surge
  → This is how pipes burst AWAY from the valve
```

### A.3 Where SWMM5 Breaks Down

SWMM5's Preissmann slot handles the transition from free-surface to pressurized flow, but it does NOT model true water hammer:

```
WHAT THE PREISSMANN SLOT DOES:
══════════════════════════════

                    Real Pipe              Preissmann Slot
                    ─────────              ───────────────
                    
Free surface:       ┌─────┐               ┌─────┐
(d < D)             │~~~~~│               │~~~~~│
                    │     │               │     │
                    └─────┘               └─────┘
                    
Surcharge:          ┌─────┐               ┌──┤├──┐  ← narrow slot
(d = D)             │█████│               │██││██│     at top
                    │█████│               │██││██│
                    └─────┘               └──┤├──┘
                    
The slot maintains a virtual free surface so the 
Saint-Venant equations still apply. The slot width 
is chosen so that the wave speed in the slot matches 
a target pressure wave speed.

Slot width: T_slot = g × A_full / c_target²

SWMM5 typically uses c_target ≈ 100-300 ft/s

WHAT THE PREISSMANN SLOT DOES NOT DO:
═════════════════════════════════════

Real acoustic wave speed in pipe: 2,000-4,000 ft/s
SWMM5 Preissmann slot wave speed: 100-300 ft/s

SWMM5 UNDERESTIMATES the speed of pressure wave 
propagation by a factor of 10-40×.

This means:
1. Pressure surges arrive at the wrong time
2. Pressure spike magnitude is WRONG 
   (Joukowsky ΔP ∝ a × ΔV — wrong a gives wrong ΔP)
3. Column separation cannot be detected 
   (requires tracking vapor pressure threshold)
4. Wave reflections from boundaries are incorrectly timed
5. Resonance effects between pump stations and tanks 
   are not captured

FOR CAPACITY ANALYSIS: SWMM5's Preissmann slot is adequate.
  It correctly determines whether a pipe surcharges, 
  how high the HGL rises, and where flooding occurs.

FOR TRANSIENT ANALYSIS: SWMM5's Preissmann slot is 
  fundamentally inadequate. The pressure magnitudes 
  and timing are physically wrong.
```

### A.4 Where EPANET Breaks Down

EPANET's quasi-steady solver doesn't even attempt to model transients:

```
WHAT EPANET DOES WITH A PUMP TRIP:
═══════════════════════════════════

Time 0:00 — Pump running, system at steady state
             EPANET solution: normal pressures everywhere ✓

Time 0:01 — Power failure, pump stops
             EPANET doesn't "see" anything between timesteps

Time 1:00 — Next hydraulic timestep
             EPANET recalculates steady state with pump OFF
             Shows: pressure dropped at nodes near pump
             Shows: tank draining to maintain service
             
WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED IN THE REAL SYSTEM:
══════════════════════════════════════════

Time 0.000 — Pump trips
Time 0.001 — Pressure wave begins propagating downstream at 3,500 ft/s
Time 0.010 — Wave has traveled 35 ft — check valve at pump slams
Time 0.050 — Wave has traveled 175 ft — pressure spike at pump discharge
Time 0.100 — Wave has traveled 350 ft — reaches first branch connection
Time 0.500 — Wave has traveled 1,750 ft — pressure oscillations 
              bouncing between pump station and tank
Time 1.000 — Wave has traveled 3,500 ft — negative pressure zone forms
              downstream of pump as momentum carries water forward
              while supply is cut off
              
              COLUMN SEPARATION may occur here.
              Pressure drops to vapor pressure (-14.5 psi).
              Vapor cavity grows.
              
Time 2.000 — Gravity/tank pressure reverses the flow.
              Vapor cavity COLLAPSES.
              Pressure spike: potentially 500+ psi.
              
              THIS IS WHEN PIPES BURST.
              
Time 5.000 — Oscillations dampening. System approaching new steady state.
Time 60.00 — System at new steady state (what EPANET would compute).

EVERYTHING between 0.000 and 60.0 seconds IS INVISIBLE TO EPANET.
The most dangerous pressures (both positive and negative) 
occur in the first 2-5 seconds — 3,600× shorter than 
EPANET's hydraulic timestep.
```

### A.5 The Method of Characteristics (MOC) — The Third Tool

The MOC solves the 1D wave equation for compressible flow in an elastic pipe:

```
GOVERNING EQUATIONS (Water Hammer):
════════════════════════════════════

∂H/∂t + (a²/gA) × ∂Q/∂x = 0              (Continuity)
∂Q/∂t + gA × ∂H/∂x + f/(2DA) × Q|Q| = 0  (Momentum)

Where:
  H = piezometric head (pressure + elevation)
  Q = flow rate
  a = acoustic wave speed (2,000-4,000 ft/s)
  g = gravitational acceleration
  A = pipe cross-sectional area
  f = Darcy-Weisbach friction factor
  D = pipe diameter

These are HYPERBOLIC PDEs — like SWMM5's Saint-Venant equations
but with the acoustic wave speed a instead of the gravity wave 
speed c = √(gA/T).

Since a >> c (3,500 >> 15 ft/s), the MOC timestep is MUCH smaller:

  Δt = Δx / a

  For a 500-ft pipe with a = 3,500 ft/s:
  Δt = 500 / 3,500 = 0.14 seconds

  Compare: SWMM5 Δt ≈ 10 seconds, EPANET Δt ≈ 3,600 seconds

THE METHOD OF CHARACTERISTICS:
═════════════════════════════

Transform the PDEs into ODEs along characteristic lines:

  Along C+ line (dx/dt = +a):
    dH + (a/gA)dQ + (f/2gDA²)Q|Q|dx = 0

  Along C- line (dx/dt = -a):
    dH - (a/gA)dQ - (f/2gDA²)Q|Q|dx = 0

These can be integrated numerically along the characteristic 
lines, giving H and Q at each pipe node at each timestep.

BOUNDARY CONDITIONS (this is where the real complexity lives):
═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  • Pump trip: torque-speed-flow relationship during rundown
    (pump inertia, motor characteristics, check valve timing)
  • Valve closure: aperture vs. time curve → head-flow relationship
  • Tank/reservoir: fixed or time-varying head boundary
  • Air valve: pressure-dependent air admission/expulsion
  • Surge tank: mass oscillation between pipe and tank
  • Air chamber: polytropic gas compression/expansion
  • PRV/PSV: pressure-dependent valve behavior during transient
  • Pipe junction: compatibility conditions between connected pipes
  • Column separation: vapor cavity formation and collapse model
```

### A.8 The Practical Workflow — Steady State to Transient

```
THE THREE-MODEL WORKFLOW FOR COMPLETE ANALYSIS
══════════════════════════════════════════════

STEP 1: Build steady-state model (EPANET or SWMM5)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  • Define network topology, pipe properties, demands
  • Calibrate to field data (pressures, flows, tank levels)
  • Validate under multiple operating conditions
  • Identify critical scenarios (pump failure, valve closure)
  • This model represents NORMAL OPERATIONS

STEP 2: Import into transient solver (MOC tool)
───────────────────────────────────────────────
  • Import EPANET .inp directly (EPA-Surge, HAMMER, TSNet)
  • Add transient-specific data:
    - Pipe material and wall thickness (wave speed)
    - Pump inertia (WR² or moment of inertia)
    - Valve closure profile (linear, parabolic, custom)
    - Check valve characteristics (closing time, slam force)
    - Surge protection devices (air chambers, surge tanks, 
      air/vacuum valves, pressure relief valves)
  • Define transient scenarios:
    - Pump trip at each pump station
    - Rapid valve closure at critical valves
    - Hydrant closure after fire flow test
    - Multiple simultaneous pump trips (worst case)

STEP 3: Run transient analysis and evaluate
──────────────────────────────────────────
  • Run MOC solver (Δt ≈ 0.001-0.1 seconds)
  • Check maximum and minimum pressures at every node:
    - Max pressure vs. pipe pressure rating
    - Min pressure vs. vapor pressure (-14.5 psi)
  • Identify locations at risk of:
    - Pipe burst (max P > rating)
    - Column separation (min P < vapor pressure)
    - Joint failure (max P > joint rating)
    - Appurtenance damage (valves, meters, etc.)
  • If unacceptable: design surge protection and rerun

STEP 4: Design surge protection (if needed)
──────────────────────────────────────────
  • Air/vacuum valves at high points (prevent column separation)
  • Surge tanks at pump stations (absorb pressure spikes)
  • Air chambers (compressed air cushion)
  • Flywheel on pump (increase rotational inertia → slower deceleration)
  • Slow-closing check valves (reduce slam pressure)
  • Variable-speed drives (controlled pump ramp-down)
  • Pressure relief valves (vent extreme pressure)
  • Rerun MOC analysis to verify protection is adequate

THE KEY INSIGHT:
════════════════
EPANET tells you WHERE the water goes and at what PRESSURE 
under normal, slowly-changing conditions.

The MOC solver tells you what EXTREME PRESSURES occur during 
rapid events that EPANET's timestep is too coarse to see.

SWMM5 tells you whether gravity pipes SURCHARGE and WHERE 
flooding occurs — but its pressure magnitudes during 
surcharge are approximate (wrong wave speed).

You need all three for a complete analysis of any system 
that has both gravity and pressurized components.
```

### B.1 The Asymmetric Impact

Climate change affects SWMM5 and EPANET models differently because the two models respond to different climate variables:

```
CLIMATE VARIABLE              SWMM5 IMPACT          EPANET IMPACT
══════════════════            ════════════           ═════════════
Precipitation intensity ↑     MAJOR — larger         Minor — may increase
                              runoff peaks,          outdoor demands
                              more flooding          slightly
                              
Precipitation frequency Δ     MAJOR — changes        Minor — affects
                              design storm return    reservoir levels
                              periods

                              evaporation, snowmelt  water demand,
                              patterns               chlorine decay rates,
                                                     bacterial growth

                              outfall tailwater,     coastal wellfield

### B.2 SWMM5 Climate Change Impacts

```
IMPACT 1: DESIGN STORM INTENSITY INCREASES
═══════════════════════════════════════════

The fundamental input to every SWMM5 model is the DESIGN STORM.
Climate change is making storms more intense:

Historical (1970s data):
  10-year, 1-hour storm: 2.5 inches
  
Current (2020s data):
  10-year, 1-hour storm: 3.0 inches (20% increase)
  
Projected (2050s):
  10-year, 1-hour storm: 3.3-3.8 inches (30-50% increase)

IMPACT ON SWMM5 MODELS:
  - Peak runoff increases nonlinearly with rainfall intensity
    (because depression storage is a fixed threshold — once exceeded,
    all additional rain becomes runoff)
  - A 20% increase in rainfall intensity can produce a 30-40% 
    increase in peak runoff for a typical urban subcatchment
  - Pipes designed for the 10-year storm using 1970s data are now 
    experiencing what is effectively a 25-50 year storm
  - Result: more frequent surcharging, flooding, CSO/SSO events

SWMM-CAT (Climate Adjustment Tool):
  EPA's tool for incorporating climate change into SWMM5
  
  1. Select location (US only)
  2. Tool accesses CMIP3/CMIP5 downscaled climate projections
  3. Generates monthly adjustment factors for:
     - Temperature
     - Evaporation
     - Rainfall intensity
  4. Factors are applied in SWMM5's [ADJUSTMENTS] section
  
  [ADJUSTMENTS]
  ;;Parameter  Jan  Feb  Mar  Apr  May  Jun  Jul  Aug  Sep  Oct  Nov  Dec
  RAINFALL    1.05 1.08 1.12 1.15 1.18 1.20 1.22 1.20 1.15 1.10 1.08 1.05
  TEMPERATURE 2.0  2.5  3.0  3.5  3.5  4.0  4.5  4.5  3.5  3.0  2.5  2.0
  EVAPORATION 1.05 1.08 1.10 1.12 1.15 1.18 1.20 1.18 1.15 1.10 1.08 1.05

IMPACT 2: SEA LEVEL RISE AND TAILWATER
═══════════════════════════════════════

For coastal communities, sea level rise directly affects 
SWMM5's outfall boundary condition:

Historical outfall tailwater (mean high tide): 3.5 ft
2050 projection: 4.5-5.5 ft (1-2 ft rise)
2100 projection: 5.5-8.5 ft (2-5 ft rise)

In SWMM5, this changes the [OUTFALLS] section:
  
  ;; Historical
  Out1  3.5  TIDAL  TidalCurve1
  
  ;; With 2 ft SLR
  Out1  5.5  TIDAL  TidalCurve1_SLR

THE CASCADE:
  Higher tailwater → reduced hydraulic gradient in trunk sewer
  → reduced pipe capacity → surcharging occurs at lower flows
  → flooding occurs during smaller storms
  → system that handled 10-year storm now floods at 5-year storm

For a trunk sewer at 0.5% slope, 48" diameter:
  Normal tailwater (3.5 ft): capacity = 45 CFS at d/D = 0.80
  With 2 ft SLR:  effective slope reduced
  Available HGL gradient reduced by ~40% near outfall
  Capacity near outfall may drop to 30-35 CFS
  
  Combined with increased rainfall intensity:
  DEMAND increases 30-40% while CAPACITY decreases 30-40%
  The system is squeezed from both ends.

IMPACT 3: INFILTRATION AND GROUNDWATER
═══════════════════════════════════════

Climate change affects SWMM5's infiltration modeling:

• More intense storms → soil surface sealing → reduced infiltration
• Higher water tables (SLR + increased precipitation) → 
  reduced infiltration capacity (Green-Ampt soil moisture deficit smaller)
• Longer droughts between storms → soil cracking → 
  preferential flow paths → initial infiltration spikes
• Temperature changes → evapotranspiration changes → 
  antecedent moisture changes → infiltration changes

For SWMM5 Green-Ampt parameters:
  Historical initial moisture deficit θd: 0.25
  With higher water table: θd may drop to 0.15-0.20
  This reduces available soil storage by 20-40%
  More runoff per event

For SWMM5 Curve Number method:
  Historical CN: 85 (for a given land use)
  With saturated antecedent conditions: effective CN → 93
  (AMC III instead of AMC II)
  If climate change makes wet antecedent conditions more common,
  the effective average CN for the watershed increases

IMPACT 4: LID PERFORMANCE DEGRADATION
═════════════════════════════════════

SWMM5's LID module assumes design-condition performance.
Climate change may degrade LID effectiveness:

Bioretention:
  • More intense storms → more overflow before infiltration
  • Higher water tables → reduced exfiltration from bottom
  • Longer droughts → soil hydrophobicity → reduced initial infiltration
  • More extreme temperatures → vegetation stress → less ET

Permeable pavement:
  • More freeze-thaw cycles → accelerated clogging
  • More intense storms → overwhelm infiltration capacity more often
  • Higher sediment loads (from construction/erosion) → faster clogging

Green roofs:
  • Higher temperatures → more ET (helps reduce runoff)
  • But also → vegetation stress/mortality (reduces ET capacity)
  • More intense storms → overflow more quickly (storage overwhelmed)

In SWMM5, this means the LID parameters need climate-adjusted values:
  Historical soil conductivity: 1.0 in/hr
  Climate-adjusted: 0.7-0.8 in/hr (higher water table, compaction)
  
  Historical drain delay: 6 hours
  Climate-adjusted: 4-5 hours (faster saturation, more bypass)
```

### B.3 EPANET Climate Change Impacts

```
IMPACT 1: WATER DEMAND INCREASES
═════════════════════════════════

Temperature is the primary driver of EPANET demand changes:

Outdoor water use (irrigation, pools, cooling):
  Increases ~1-3% per 1°F temperature rise
  For 5°F warming by 2060: 5-15% demand increase
  
Peak day demand increases MORE than average:
  Historical peaking factor (max day/avg day): 1.5
  Climate-adjusted peaking factor: 1.6-1.8
  
  This means the DESIGN SCENARIO gets worse:
  Max day + fire flow is the sizing condition for pipes and pumps.
  Higher max day = larger pipes needed = more pump capacity.

  Summer  1.5  1.4  1.3  1.4  1.5  1.4
  Summer  1.2  1.0  0.8  0.6  0.5  0.4
  
  ;; Climate-adjusted pattern (more irrigation, higher peak)
  Summer  0.5  0.4  0.4  0.5  0.7  0.9
  Summer  1.1  1.4  1.5  1.6  1.8  1.9  ;; higher peak
  Summer  1.8  1.7  1.5  1.6  1.8  1.6

IMPACT 2: WATER QUALITY DEGRADATION
════════════════════════════════════

Temperature directly affects EPANET's water quality modeling:

Chlorine bulk decay:
  kb increases ~50% per 10°C temperature rise (Arrhenius)
  
  At 15°C: kb = 0.5/day → chlorine half-life = 1.4 days
  At 25°C: kb = 0.75/day → chlorine half-life = 0.9 days
  At 35°C: kb = 1.1/day → chlorine half-life = 0.6 days
  
  Higher temperatures → faster chlorine decay → 
  more dead-end nodes fall below minimum residual →
  more flushing needed → more water wasted

Chlorine wall decay:
  kw increases with temperature and biofilm activity
  Warmer pipe walls → more biofilm growth → faster chlorine consumption
  
  This is an EPANET-specific concern — SWMM5 has no wall reaction model.

Bacterial regrowth:
  Warmer temperatures → more biofilm growth in pipes
  Combined with lower chlorine → potential for:
    - Total coliform violations
    - Legionella growth (particularly in building plumbing)
    - Disinfection byproduct formation (longer treatment needed)

Water age:
  If demand patterns shift (more peak demand → more off-peak storage),
  water age in tanks and dead ends may increase.
  
  EPANET water age analysis becomes MORE important under climate change
  because the consequences of high water age are more severe.

In EPANET [REACTIONS]:
  ;; Historical
  GLOBAL BULK   -0.5    ;; kb = 0.5/day at 15°C
  GLOBAL WALL   -0.5    ;; kw = 0.5 ft/day
  
  ;; Climate-adjusted (warmer water, more biofilm)
  GLOBAL BULK   -0.8    ;; kb = 0.8/day at 20°C average
  GLOBAL WALL   -0.8    ;; kw = 0.8 ft/day (more biofilm)

IMPACT 3: SOURCE WATER CHANGES
══════════════════════════════

Climate change affects the reservoirs that feed EPANET models:

Surface water sources:
  • More intense precipitation → more turbidity events → 
    treatment plant may shut down temporarily
  • Longer droughts → reservoir levels drop → 
    may need to activate backup sources
  • Algal blooms (warmer, more nutrients) → 
    taste/odor/toxin issues requiring treatment changes
  • Source water quality changes → treatment chemical doses change →
    different initial chlorine concentrations entering the system

Groundwater sources:
  • Saltwater intrusion (coastal, from SLR) → 
    wells may become unusable → supply reduction
  • Changing recharge patterns → water table changes → 
    well capacity changes
  • Higher temperatures → increased demand → aquifer depletion

In EPANET, source changes appear as:
  [RESERVOIRS]
  ;; Historical: two surface water sources
  Res1  250   ;; Treatment Plant 1, always available
  Res2  245   ;; Treatment Plant 2, always available
  
  ;; Climate scenario: intermittent availability
  Res1  250   ;; Available
  Res2  245   ;; Closed due to algal bloom (modeled by removing)
  ;; → System must operate on single source
  ;; → Pressures drop, water age increases, 
  ;;   blending zones shift dramatically

IMPACT 4: ENERGY AND COST IMPLICATIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════

EPANET's energy analysis is directly affected:

Higher demands → more pumping → higher energy costs
Higher peak demands → higher peak kW charges
More extreme temperatures → cooling water demands for treatment plants

But also opportunities:
  Time-of-use electricity pricing + climate variability →
  More sophisticated pump scheduling optimization
  
  EPANET's pump energy analysis becomes MORE valuable 
  under climate change because the cost of suboptimal 
  pumping increases with demand.
```

   Apply SWMM-CAT adjustment factors

## C. DIGITAL TWINS — REAL-TIME SWMM5 AND EPANET

### C.1 What Is a Digital Twin in Water Infrastructure?

```
DIGITAL TWIN ARCHITECTURE
═════════════════════════

PHYSICAL SYSTEM                 DIGITAL TWIN
══════════════                  ════════════
                                
Pipes, valves,    ←── Sensors ──→  Hydraulic Model
pumps, tanks          (SCADA,      (EPANET or SWMM5)
                       IoT,            ↕
                       AMI)       Data Assimilation
                                  (model updating)
                                      ↕
                                  Predictive Engine
                                  (forecast next hours/days)
                                      ↕
                                  Decision Support
                                  (optimize operations)
                                      ↕
Actuators         ←── Commands ──  Control Actions
(pump VFDs,                       (pump schedules,
 valve motors,                     valve setpoints,
 gates)                            alerts)

THE FOUR LEVELS OF DIGITAL TWIN MATURITY:
═════════════════════════════════════════

Level 1: DESCRIPTIVE
  "What is happening now?"
  Static model + SCADA dashboard
  No model updating
  Manual analysis
  
Level 2: DIAGNOSTIC  
  "Why is it happening?"
  Calibrated model + real-time data comparison
  Anomaly detection (model vs. reality mismatch)
  Example: leak detection (pressure drop not explained by model)
  
Level 3: PREDICTIVE
  "What will happen next?"
  Real-time model updating + forecast
  Predict: tank levels in 6 hours, CSO in 3 hours
  Enable proactive operations
  
Level 4: PRESCRIPTIVE
  "What should we do?"
  Optimization engine on top of predictive model
  Compute optimal pump schedules, valve settings
  Automatic control recommendations or direct actuation
```

### C.2 EPANET Digital Twins

EPANET is well-suited to digital twin applications because of its speed and stability:

```
EPANET DIGITAL TWIN WORKFLOW
═════════════════════════════

DATA INPUTS (from SCADA/IoT/AMI):
──────────────────────────────────
• Tank levels (every 1-5 minutes)
• Pump status (on/off) and speed (VFD setting)
• Valve positions (open/closed, PRV setpoint)
• Flow meters (at plant discharge, zone boundaries, DMAs)
• Pressure sensors (at critical nodes, 5-50 throughout system)
• AMI meters (hourly reads from every customer)
• Water quality (online chlorine analyzers, turbidity)

MODEL UPDATING (Data Assimilation):
───────────────────────────────────
Every 5-15 minutes:

1. Read current SCADA data
2. Set EPANET boundary conditions:
   - Tank initial levels from SCADA
   - Pump statuses from SCADA
   - Valve settings from SCADA
   - Reservoir heads from SCADA (if variable)
3. Estimate current demands:
   - AMI data (if available) → direct demand at each meter
   - OR: scale demand patterns by zone using DMA flow meters
   - OR: use ML-predicted demands based on time, temperature, 
         day of week
4. Run EPANET simulation (< 1 second)
5. Compare modeled pressures to measured pressures
6. If mismatch > threshold → possible anomaly:
   - Leak (modeled P > measured P)
   - Closed valve (modeled flow ≠ measured flow)
   - Demand error (systematic P offset)
7. Adjust demands or pipe status to minimize residuals
8. Publish results: pressure map, flow map, water quality map

PREDICTION:
───────────
Run EPANET forward 6-24 hours using:
  - Forecasted demand patterns (based on weather, day type)
  - Current tank levels as initial conditions
  - Planned pump/valve schedules

Predict:
  - Tank levels (will any go empty or overflow?)
  - Pressures (will any drop below minimum?)
  - Water age (which zones will exceed targets?)
  - Energy cost (what will pumping cost today?)

OPTIMIZATION:
─────────────
Find the pump schedule that minimizes energy cost while:
  - Maintaining all pressures above minimum
  - Keeping all tanks within operating range
  - Maintaining minimum chlorine residual everywhere

This is a mixed-integer optimization (pumps on/off = integer):
  - Solved using GA, dynamic programming, or heuristics
  - EPANET runs thousands of scenarios in minutes
  - Result: optimal pump schedule for next 24 hours

REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES:
════════════════════

EPANET-RTX (EPA + University of Cincinnati):
  Real-time hydraulic modeling framework based on EPANET
  Developed by EPA and UC researchers
  Open-source extension of EPANET for real-time applications
  Ingests SCADA data, runs EPANET, publishes results

Xylem Vue powered by GoAigua:
  "Network Real-Time Decision Support"
  Creates a network digital twin using hydraulic models
  Calibrated and optimized via machine learning
  Used by Nashville Metro Water Services (MWS):
    - Discovered high water age in a tank
    - Changed operational strategy based on DT data
    - Confirmed improvement through continued monitoring

Valencia, Spain:
  Pioneered EPANET + GIS + telemetry integration
  One of the earliest digital twin implementations for WDN
  "Building and exploiting a Digital Twin for the management 
   of drinking water distribution networks" (Fuertes et al., 2020)

DHALSIM (SUTD / TU Delft / CISPA):
  Digital Hardware-in-the-Loop Simulator
  Uses WNTR (EPANET wrapper) for hydraulic simulation
  Emulates industrial control system behavior
  Used for cybersecurity research on water infrastructure
```

### C.3 SWMM5 Digital Twins

SWMM5 digital twins are more challenging due to runtime constraints:

```
SWMM5 DIGITAL TWIN CHALLENGES
══════════════════════════════

CHALLENGE 1: RUNTIME
  EPANET: 10,000-node model runs in < 1 second
  SWMM5:  10,000-element model takes 5-30 minutes (DW routing)
  
  For real-time application (update every 5 minutes):
    EPANET: easily feasible — runs 300× per update interval
    SWMM5: barely feasible — may not finish before next update
    
  Solutions:
  a. Simplified models (fewer elements, kinematic wave)
  b. Faster hardware (GPU acceleration, parallel computing)
  c. Surrogate models (ML trained on SWMM5 outputs)
  d. Hybrid: run detailed SWMM5 offline, simple model in real-time

CHALLENGE 2: RAINFALL NOWCASTING
  SWMM5 needs FUTURE rainfall to predict system behavior
  Rain gages measure CURRENT rainfall
  Radar and NWP models provide 0-6 hour forecasts
  Forecast uncertainty increases rapidly with lead time
  
  Ensemble approach:
    Run SWMM5 with 20-50 rainfall forecast scenarios
    Report probability of flooding: "70% chance of flooding 
    at Junction J15 in the next 2 hours"
    
  But 20 SWMM5 runs × 5 min each = 100 min total runtime
  → Must use simplified models or surrogates

CHALLENGE 3: STATE ESTIMATION
  EPANET state estimation is straightforward:
    Tank levels from SCADA → initial conditions → run model
    
  SWMM5 state estimation is complex:
    What is the current depth at every junction and in every pipe?
    Most are NOT monitored.
    Must estimate from sparse flow/depth monitors.
    The nonlinear, transient nature of SWMM5 makes this much harder
    than EPANET's quasi-steady state estimation.

SWMM5 DIGITAL TWIN WORKFLOW
════════════════════════════

DATA INPUTS:
────────────
• Rain gages (1-5 minute intervals)
• Radar rainfall estimates (gridded, 5-15 min resolution)
• Flow monitors in key manholes (5-15 minute intervals)
• Level monitors in storage facilities
• Pump station SCADA (pump status, wet well levels)
• Gate/valve positions (if real-time controlled)
• Tidal data (for coastal outfalls)
• Radar rainfall FORECASTS (0-6 hour lead time)

MODEL UPDATING:
───────────────
Every 5-15 minutes:

1. Ingest current and recent rainfall data
2. Update SWMM5 rain gages with observed rainfall
3. Run SWMM5 from last known state to current time
4. Compare modeled flows/depths to monitored values
5. If mismatch:
   - Adjust subcatchment parameters (real-time calibration)
   - OR: apply correction factors to modeled results
   - OR: use Kalman filter / ensemble adjustment
6. Publish current system state: depths, flows, capacity utilization

PREDICTION:
───────────
1. Obtain rainfall forecast (radar extrapolation, NWP model)
2. Generate ensemble of possible future rainfall scenarios
3. Run SWMM5 forward for each scenario (1-6 hours)
4. Report:
   - Probability of surcharging at each junction
   - Expected flooding volumes and locations
   - CSO activation probability
   - Time to peak flow at outfall

CONTROL:
────────
For systems with real-time control (RTC):
1. Based on predictions, compute optimal control actions:
   - Gate positions to maximize in-system storage
   - Pump operations to pre-drain storage ahead of storm
   - Diversion settings to minimize CSO volume
2. Send commands to SCADA/PLC
3. Monitor response and adjust

REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES:

## D. MACHINE LEARNING + SWMM5/EPANET

### D.1 Why ML Surrogate Models?

```
THE PROBLEM THAT ML SOLVES
══════════════════════════

Many water engineering problems require THOUSANDS of model runs:

• Optimization: Find the best pipe sizes, pump schedules, 
  LID placement → need 1,000-100,000 runs to search the space

• Uncertainty analysis: How does output change when inputs 
  are uncertain? → Monte Carlo with 10,000+ runs

• Real-time control: What's the optimal action RIGHT NOW?
  → need answer in seconds, not minutes

• Calibration: Find parameters that match observations
  → need 1,000-10,000 trial-and-error runs

EPANET: Each run takes < 1 second
  → 10,000 runs = 3 hours → FEASIBLE without ML
  → BUT for very large networks or complex optimization: ML helps

SWMM5 (Dynamic Wave): Each run takes 5-30 minutes  
  → 10,000 runs = 35-208 DAYS → INFEASIBLE without ML
  → Surrogate model: train ML on 500-1,000 SWMM5 runs,
    then use ML for the remaining 9,000+ evaluations
  → ML inference: < 0.01 seconds per evaluation
  → 10,000 ML evaluations = 100 seconds → FEASIBLE
```

### D.2 Surrogate Model Architectures

```
ARCHITECTURE 1: FEEDFORWARD NEURAL NETWORK (ANN/MLP)
═════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Input layer:        Rainfall depth, duration, antecedent conditions,
                    subcatchment parameters
Hidden layers:      2-5 layers, 32-256 neurons each
Output layer:       Peak flow, total volume, flooding volume,
                    max depth at critical nodes

Training data:      500-2,000 SWMM5 simulations
                    Latin Hypercube Sampling of input space

Performance:        R² > 0.90 for peak flow
                    R² > 0.95 for total volume
                    100-1,000× speedup over SWMM5

BEST FOR:          Global output prediction (system-level metrics)
                   Optimization (pipe sizing, LID placement)
                   Uncertainty analysis (Monte Carlo)

LIMITATION:        Doesn't predict SPATIAL distribution well
                   Doesn't capture time series (hydrograph shape)

ARCHITECTURE 2: LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)
═════════════════════════════════════════════

Input sequence:     Rainfall time series (5-min intervals)
                    + static parameters (subcatchment properties)
Hidden state:       64-256 LSTM units, 1-4 layers
Output sequence:    Flow/depth time series at each node

Training data:      1,000-5,000 SWMM5 simulations
                    Multiple storm events, varying parameters

Performance:        NSE > 0.85 for hydrograph prediction
                    R² > 0.80 for extreme events
                    92.6% reduction in computational time
                    (from ScienceDirect search results)

BEST FOR:          Temporal prediction (hydrograph shape)
                   Real-time forecasting (feed current data in)
                   Node-level water depth and inflow prediction

RECENT ADVANCE:    SWMM-LSTM surrogate (from ResearchGate results)
                   "Spatiotemporal SWMM-LSTM surrogate modeling 
                   for efficient node-level water depth and inflow 
                   prediction in urban drainage networks"
                   
                   Trained on 5,000 rainfall events across 7 ARIs
                   Hyperparameter optimization via grid search
                   Task-specific configs: larger hidden layers for 
                   depth, deeper networks for inflow

LIMITATION:        Requires extensive training data
                   May not generalize to events outside training range
                   Spatial relationships not explicitly modeled

ARCHITECTURE 3: GRAPH NEURAL NETWORK (GNN)
══════════════════════════════════════════

Input:              Node features (elevation, demand, subcatch params)
                    Edge features (pipe diameter, length, roughness)
                    Graph structure (network topology)
                    + temporal input (rainfall or demand time series)

Architecture:       Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)
                    or Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN)
                    Nodes exchange information along edges
                    Multiple message-passing rounds capture
                    multi-hop hydraulic interactions

Output:             Pressure/depth at every node
                    Flow in every pipe

Performance:        Promising but still research-stage
                    Good at capturing SPATIAL correlations
                    Network topology is EXPLICITLY encoded

BEST FOR:          Pressure estimation in EPANET networks
                   (from search results: "Graph Neural Networks 
                   for Pressure Estimation in Water Distribution 
                   Systems" — Truong et al., Water Resources Research)
                   Anomaly detection (leak identification)
                   Transfer learning between similar networks

KEY ADVANTAGE:     The GNN architecture MATCHES the network topology.
                   For EPANET's looped networks, the graph structure
                   naturally captures the simultaneous flow 
                   redistribution that the Gradient Algorithm computes.
                   
                   For SWMM5's tree networks, the GNN's message 
                   passing mimics the upstream→downstream flow 
                   routing — but the tree structure is simpler.

LIMITATION:        Training requires many simulations
                   Scaling to very large networks is challenging
                   Transient dynamics harder than steady-state
```

### D.3 Does Topology Affect Which ML Architecture Works Best?

```
YES — THE TOPOLOGY-ML CONNECTION
════════════════════════════════

TREE TOPOLOGY (SWMM5):
──────────────────────
• Information flows UPSTREAM → DOWNSTREAM
• Each node is affected by its UPSTREAM subcatchments
• The "receptive field" is the subtree above each node
• TIME is the dominant dimension (hydrograph evolution)

BEST ML ARCHITECTURES FOR TREES:
  1. LSTM — captures temporal evolution (hydrograph routing)
  2. 1D CNN — captures temporal patterns in rainfall-runoff
  3. Tree-structured LSTM — natural match for dendritic networks
  4. GNN with directed edges — message passing follows flow direction

WHY: The tree structure means information has a clear 
directional flow. Sequential architectures (LSTM, 1D CNN) 
naturally model this. The spatial dimension is secondary 
because each branch is hydraulically independent 
(under non-surcharge conditions).

LOOP TOPOLOGY (EPANET):
───────────────────────
• Information flows ALL DIRECTIONS simultaneously
• Each node is affected by EVERY other node (through loops)
• The "receptive field" is the ENTIRE network
• SPACE is the dominant dimension (pressure distribution)

BEST ML ARCHITECTURES FOR LOOPS:
  1. GNN — natural match for graph-structured data
  2. Graph Attention Network (GAT) — learns which neighbors matter most
  3. Spatial-temporal GNN — combines graph structure with time
  4. Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) — embeds conservation laws

WHY: The looped structure means every node depends on 
every other node. GNN's message-passing mechanism naturally 
captures this simultaneous interaction. The temporal dimension 
is secondary because EPANET finds equilibrium at each timestep.

COMPARISON:
                    TREE (SWMM5)           LOOP (EPANET)
Dominant dimension: TIME                   SPACE
Information flow:   Sequential (U/S→D/S)   Simultaneous (all dirs)
Best architecture:  LSTM / temporal CNN    GNN / graph attention
Training data need: 1,000-5,000 events    500-2,000 scenarios
Prediction target:  Hydrograph at nodes    Pressure at nodes
Generalization:     Hard (weather-dependent) Easier (demand-dependent)
Transfer learning:  Difficult (site-specific Promising (similar
                    hydrology)              network structures)
```

## E. THE FORCE MAIN DEEP DIVE

### E.1 Force Mains: The Boundary Between SWMM5 and EPANET

```
A FORCE MAIN IS A PRESSURIZED PIPE IN A GRAVITY SYSTEM.

It is the PHYSICAL EMBODIMENT of the SWMM5/EPANET boundary.

Upstream: gravity flow → SWMM5 domain (free surface, Manning's)
Force main: pressurized → hybrid domain (full pipe, H-W or D-W)
Downstream: gravity flow → SWMM5 domain again (free surface, Manning's)

    Gravity Sewer → Wet Well → PUMP → Force Main → Gravity Sewer
    (SWMM5 world)   (SWMM5)   (SWMM5)  (????)    (SWMM5 world)
                                          ↑
                                    THIS IS THE 
                                    CONTESTED ZONE
```

### E.2 When to Use SWMM5 vs. EPANET for Force Mains

```
USE SWMM5 WHEN:
═══════════════

1. The force main is PART OF A LARGER COLLECTION SYSTEM
   - Upstream gravity sewers feed the pump station
   - You need to model the interaction between gravity flow 
     and the pump station's cycling behavior
   - The wet well level (SWMM5 storage node) determines 
     when the pump turns on/off
   - Example: a typical lift station in a sanitary sewer system

2. The force main DRAINS DOWN between pump cycles
   - When the pump turns off, the force main partially drains
   - Water level drops from full to partially full
   - SWMM5's dynamic wave handles the gravity → pressure → 
     gravity transition within the force main itself
   - This is where the Preissmann slot and equivalent n 
     conversion actually matter

3. You need to model PUMP CYCLING dynamics
   - Pump on → force main fills → flow to downstream junction
   - Pump off → force main drains → no flow for a period
   - Wet well refills → pump turns on again
   - The cycling frequency depends on inflow rate, wet well 
     volume, pump capacity, and force main resistance
   - SWMM5 handles this natively with pump control rules

4. There are MULTIPLE PUMP STATIONS feeding into each other
   - Pump Station A → Force Main A → Gravity → 
     Pump Station B → Force Main B → Gravity → Outfall
   - The interaction between stations (B's inflow depends 
     on A's discharge) requires modeling the entire system
   - SWMM5 handles this as one connected model

5. You need to model SURCHARGE conditions in the gravity system
   - If the downstream gravity sewer surcharges, it affects 
     the force main discharge conditions
   - SWMM5's dynamic wave captures this backwater effect

USE EPANET WHEN:
═══════════════

1. The force main is a STANDALONE pressurized system
   - Multiple pump stations feeding a common force main
   - The force main behaves like a small distribution system
   - No gravity sewer interaction needed

2. You need DETAILED PRESSURE ANALYSIS
   - Pressure at high points (check for column separation risk)
   - Pressure at low points (check for excessive pressure)
   - EPANET gives you pressure at every node directly
   - SWMM5 gives you depth in the pipe, which you must 
     convert to pressure (less intuitive)

3. You need STEADY-STATE pump operating point analysis
   - System head curve vs. pump curve intersection
   - Multiple pumps in parallel: how do they share flow?
   - EPANET's pump modeling is more intuitive for this
   
4. Multiple pump stations compete for the same force main
   - Three pump stations pumping into a manifolded force main
   - EPANET's simultaneous solver handles this naturally
   - SWMM5 can do it but may be less stable for 
     complex multi-pump configurations

5. The force main is ALWAYS FULL (pump runs continuously)
   - No drain-down, no partial flow transition
   - The system is always in EPANET's domain (full, pressurized)
   - Using SWMM5 adds unnecessary complexity (Preissmann slot 
     for a pipe that never needs it)

6. From practitioner experience (OpenSWMM forum discussions):
   "For sanitary pump stations I tend towards EPAnet based programs.
    Partly because of the dent still in my forehead from SWMM4 days
    and because the reporting/visualization features of EPAnet are 
    better suited for pressure systems. It also allows modelling 
    of minor losses for bends, check valves, reducers, etc. to be 
    a little easier. I have also found for multipump systems to be 
    a little bit more stable (even for SWMM5)."
```

### E.3 SWMM5 Force Main Setup

```
STEP-BY-STEP SWMM5 FORCE MAIN CONFIGURATION
═════════════════════════════════════════════

STEP 1: Choose Force Main Equation
───────────────────────────────────
[OPTIONS]
FORCE_MAIN_EQUATION  H-W    ;; Hazen-Williams (most common)
;;FORCE_MAIN_EQUATION  D-W  ;; Darcy-Weisbach (more rigorous)

This is a GLOBAL setting. All force mains in the model 
use the same equation. Cannot mix H-W and D-W.

STEP 2: Define the Wet Well (Storage Node)
──────────────────────────────────────────
[STORAGE]
;;Name   Elev   MaxD  InitD  Shape    Curve/Params
WetWell  85.0   15.0  5.0    FUNCTIONAL  50  0  0
;; 50 ft² constant area, initial depth 5 ft

STEP 3: Define the Pump
───────────────────────
[PUMPS]
;;Name   From      To      Curve    Status  Startup  Shutoff
Pump1    WetWell   FM_Up   PCurve1  ON      8.0      2.0
;; Pump turns ON when wet well depth > 8 ft
;; Pump turns OFF when wet well depth < 2 ft

[CURVES]
;;Name   Type   X-Value  Y-Value
PCurve1  PUMP3  0        50       ;; Q=0, H=50 ft (shutoff head)
PCurve1         100      45       ;; Q=100 GPM, H=45 ft
PCurve1         200      35       ;; Q=200 GPM, H=35 ft
PCurve1         300      20       ;; Q=300 GPM, H=20 ft
PCurve1         350      0        ;; Q=350 GPM, H=0 (max flow)

STEP 4: Define the Force Main Conduit
─────────────────────────────────────
[CONDUITS]
;;Name   From    To       Length  Roughness  InOff  OutOff  InitFlow
FM1      FM_Up   FM_Down  2000   130        0      0       0
;; Roughness = 130 because FORCE_MAIN_EQUATION = H-W
;; If D-W: roughness would be ε in project length units

[XSECTIONS]
;;Link   Shape       Geom1  Geom2  Geom3  Geom4
FM1      FORCE_MAIN  1.0    0      0      0
;; 1.0 ft (12 inch) diameter
;; MUST be FORCE_MAIN shape, NOT CIRCULAR

STEP 5: Define the Discharge Junction
─────────────────────────────────────
[JUNCTIONS]
;;Name    Elev    MaxD    InitD   SurD    PondedArea
FM_Up     85.0    20.0    0       0       0
FM_Down   80.0    15.0    0       0       0

STEP 6: Add Control Rules (if not using startup/shutoff depths)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[CONTROLS]
RULE PumpOn
IF NODE WetWell DEPTH > 8.0
THEN PUMP Pump1 STATUS = ON

RULE PumpOff
IF NODE WetWell DEPTH < 2.0
THEN PUMP Pump1 STATUS = OFF

COMMON MISTAKES AND FIXES:
══════════════════════════

MISTAKE 1: Using CIRCULAR instead of FORCE_MAIN shape
  SWMM5 will use Manning's equation for ALL flow conditions.
  The H-W pressurized equation will NEVER be applied.
  FIX: Change [XSECTIONS] shape to FORCE_MAIN.

MISTAKE 2: Entering Manning's n as roughness for H-W force main
  If FORCE_MAIN_EQUATION = H-W, roughness field = C value (not n).
  Entering 0.013 (Manning's n) instead of 130 (H-W C) will give 
  WILDLY wrong headloss (off by factor of 10,000).
  FIX: Enter appropriate C value (60-150 range).

MISTAKE 3: Force main doesn't stay full
  The downstream node depth is too low → force main reports 
  d/D < 1.0 even when pump is running.
  FIX: 
  a. Increase downstream node maximum depth
  b. Add a break node near the discharge end with high max depth
  c. Check that downstream gravity pipe can accept the pump flow

MISTAKE 4: Pump cycling too fast (hunting)
  Pump turns on, fills wet well's available volume quickly,
  triggers off depth, then on again in seconds.
  FIX: Increase wet well storage volume, or widen the 
  on/off depth differential (startup = 8 ft, shutoff = 2 ft,
  not startup = 5 ft, shutoff = 4 ft).

MISTAKE 5: High continuity error during pump cycling
  When the pump turns on/off, the rapid flow change in the 
  force main can violate the Courant condition.
  FIX: Reduce routing timestep, or use SWMM5's variable 
  timestep option with a lower Courant safety factor.
```

### E.4 Multi-Pump Force Main Systems

```
THE COMPLEX CASE: THREE PUMP STATIONS, ONE FORCE MAIN
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Station A ──→ FM_A ──┐
                      ├──→ Common Force Main ──→ WWTP
Station B ──→ FM_B ──┤
                      │
Station C ──→ FM_C ──┘

HYDRAULIC ISSUES:
─────────────────

1. PUMP INTERACTION:
   When Station A pumps, it raises the HGL in the common force main.
   This increases the head that Station B must pump against.
   If B turns on while A is running, A's flow DECREASES 
   (operating point shifts on pump curve).
   
   Three pumps running: each pump delivers less than it would alone.
   System curve shifts UP with each additional pump.

2. MANIFOLD PRESSURE BALANCE:
   At the junction of FM_A, FM_B, FM_C and the common FM:
   All three force mains must have the SAME pressure at the junction.
   The flow from each station depends on its pump curve and 
   the common manifold pressure.
   This is a SIMULTANEOUS system — exactly EPANET's domain.

3. CHECK VALVE BEHAVIOR:
   When Station A is off but B and C are running, 
   the common FM pressure may push water BACK toward A.
   A check valve prevents this — but the check valve's 
   behavior during transients (slam, flutter) may be important.

MODELING APPROACH:
──────────────────

EPANET approach (simpler, recommended for steady-state):
  - Each pump station = Reservoir (head = wet well level) + Pump + Pipe
  - Common FM = pipe segments with junctions at connections
  - Check valves = pipe status = CV (check valve)
  - Analyze: what is each pump's flow when various combinations 
    of 1, 2, or 3 pumps are running?
  - Easy to generate system curves and find operating points.

SWMM5 approach (necessary for pump cycling dynamics):
  - Each pump station = Storage node (wet well) + Pump + Force Main conduit
  - Common FM = Force Main conduits connected at junction
  - Control rules for each pump based on wet well levels
  - Analyze: what are the pump cycling patterns? How do they interact?
  - Captures the dynamic cycling behavior that EPANET can't model.

HYBRID approach (best of both worlds):
  - Use EPANET for initial design (pipe sizing, pump selection)
  - Use SWMM5 for operational analysis (pump cycling, wet weather)
  - Use MOC tool for transient analysis (pump trip, check valve slam)
```

### E.5 Air Issues in Force Mains

```
THE AIR PROBLEM — NEITHER SWMM5 NOR EPANET HANDLES WELL
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Force mains trap air at high points in the profile.
Air causes:
  - Reduced flow capacity (air pocket restricts cross-section)
  - Pressure surges (compressed air acts as a spring)
  - Check valve flutter (air/water slug flow)
  - Pump efficiency loss (air in pump casing)
  - Corrosion (H₂S accumulation at air pockets in sewage FM)

SWMM5 LIMITATION:
  Cannot model air pockets explicitly.
  The Preissmann slot assumes the pipe is either 
  water-filled or has a virtual free surface — 
  not a trapped air pocket at a high point.

EPANET LIMITATION:
  Cannot model air pockets. Pipes are always full of water.
  No two-phase flow capability.

WHAT'S NEEDED:
  A two-phase (air-water) flow model.
  Few commercial tools handle this well.
  Academic models exist (e.g., Illinois Transient Model — ITM)
  but are not widely used in practice.

PRACTICAL APPROACH:
  1. Identify high points in force main profile
  2. Install air/vacuum valves at each high point
  3. Size air valves using manufacturer guidance
     (based on pipe diameter, flow rate, expected air volume)
  4. In SWMM5/EPANET: assume air valves work perfectly
     (no air accumulation)
  5. For critical systems: use transient solver to verify 
     air valve performance during pump trip / startup

From the search results (LID Conference accepted abstracts):
  "Applying Epanet to Represent Air Phase Flows During 
   Water Distribution Network Priming"
  — Vitor G. Geller and Jose G.
  
  This suggests emerging research on using EPANET to model 
  air phase behavior — a topic at the frontier of both models.
```

### E.6 The Gravity-Pressure Transition

```
THE MOST NUMERICALLY CHALLENGING POINT IN ALL OF HYDRAULICS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

When a force main transitions between partially full and full:

PUMP OFF → DRAIN-DOWN:
─────────────────────
Full pipe ─→ Water level drops below crown ─→ Free surface forms
           ↑
           THIS INSTANT is where:
           • Top width T → 0 (from full pipe side)
           • Top width T → 0 (as surface appears)
           • Wave speed c = √(gA/T) → ∞ then jumps to finite value
           • SWMM5's Preissmann slot activates/deactivates
           • Manning's ↔ H-W equation switch occurs
           • The equivalent n conversion kicks in

PUMP ON → FILL-UP:
──────────────────
Partially full ─→ Water level reaches crown ─→ Pipe pressurizes
                 ↑
                 THIS INSTANT is where:
                 • Bore front (pressurization wave) propagates
                 • Can create pressure spikes (waterhammer-like)
                 • Air ahead of the bore must be vented
                 • SWMM5 handles this with Preissmann slot
                 • But the bore speed in SWMM5 (~100-300 ft/s) 
                   may be wrong (real bore: 20-2,000 ft/s 
                   depending on conditions)

SWMM5's HANDLING:
────────────────
The Preissmann slot provides a continuous transition:
  Depth < D: Free surface, Manning's equation, T = f(depth)
  Depth = D: Slot activates, T = T_slot (very narrow)
  Depth > D: Surcharged, virtual free surface in slot
  
The equivalent n ensures that at d/D = 1.0, both Manning's 
and H-W give the SAME friction slope — a smooth handoff.
This is what the SWMM5 forcemain.c code computes.

FOR CAPACITY ANALYSIS: This transition handling is ADEQUATE.
  You get the right answer for: does the force main surcharge?
  How high does the HGL rise? What is the pump operating point?

FOR TRANSIENT ANALYSIS: This transition handling is APPROXIMATE.
  The bore speed, pressure spike magnitude, and air behavior 
  during rapid filling are not accurately captured.
  Need MOC-based transient solver for these effects.
```
