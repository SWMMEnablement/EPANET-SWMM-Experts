SWMM5 — Your World

Gravity-driven, open-channel flow

Pipes are partially full most of the time. The free surface means atmospheric pressure acts on the water. Flow is driven by the slope of the conduit and the hydraulic gradient. The depth of water varies along the pipe and over time.

You think about invert elevations, crown elevations, and whether a pipe is surcharged.

EPANET — New World

Pressure-driven, full-pipe flow

Pipes are always full. There is no free surface — the entire cross section is water under pressure. Flow is driven by pressure differences between nodes, created by pumps and the elevation of tanks/reservoirs.

You think about pressure heads, hydraulic grade lines, and whether pressure drops below minimum service levels.

💡 Aha Moment

Head = Elevation + Pressure (no more "depth")

In SWMM, head at a junction = invert elevation + water depth. In EPANET, head at a node = elevation + pressure head. The concept of "head" is identical — it's the energy per unit weight of water. You already understand this from reading HGL profiles in SWMM. In EPANET, the HGL is everything.

🔑 Key Difference

No surcharge mode — pipes are ALWAYS full

In SWMM, surcharging is an exception — something you worry about and design against. In EPANET, what you'd call "surcharged" is the normal and only operating condition. There's no concept of a partially-full pipe. If a pipe has water in it, it's full, period.

SWMM Junction properties: Invert elevation (bottom of pipe), maximum depth (surcharge limit), initial depth, surcharge depth, and ponded area (surface flooding storage). The junction is where pipes connect — water rises and falls within it based on inflow/outflow balance.

EPANET Junction properties: Elevation (ground level or pipe centerline — NOT invert), base demand (flow withdrawal in GPM/LPS), demand pattern (time-varying multiplier), and emitter coefficient (pressure-dependent outflow). There is no max depth, no ponded area, no initial depth — because junctions have zero storage volume.

The critical difference: A SWMM junction stores water and has a water level that rises and falls. An EPANET junction is just a mathematical point where pipes connect and flow is balanced — it has no volume. This is why SWMM needs tiny timesteps (the twitchy surface area problem) while EPANET can use hour-long steps.

SWMM Storage: Any shape via a functional curve (area vs. depth) or tabular data. Supports evaporation, seepage (exfiltration), and treatment functions (pollutant removal equations). The shape flexibility is powerful — you can model irregular ponds, basins, and underground vaults.

EPANET Tank: Cylindrical (diameter + min/max levels) OR a volume curve for non-cylindrical shapes. Has initial level, minimum level (won't drain below), and maximum level (won't fill above). Supports reaction coefficients for water quality decay within the tank.

What's new — Mixing Models: EPANET tanks have four mixing models for water quality that SWMM has no equivalent for:

• Complete Mix — incoming water instantly blends with all stored water (default, like a CSTR reactor)

• Two-Compartment — an inlet/outlet zone that mixes fully, and a dead zone that exchanges slowly. Models short-circuiting in large tanks.

• FIFO (First-In, First-Out) — plug flow, like a long narrow tank. Water travels through without mixing. Oldest water exits first.

• LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) — newest water exits first, like a tank where inlet and outlet are at the same location. Oldest water stagnates at the bottom.

These mixing models dramatically affect water age and chlorine residual predictions. SWMM storage nodes use complete mixing only.

Key limitation: All four EPANET mixing models are zero-dimensional (lumped volume) — there is no spatial variation within the tank. EPANET tanks also have no horizontal inflow/outflow momentum effects; the tank is purely a level-driven storage element. By contrast, SWMM storage units can capture inflow momentum and irregular geometry effects on routing, which may matter for large basins.

SWMM Conduit: Any cross-section shape — circular, rectangular (closed/open), trapezoidal, triangular, parabolic, power, horseshoe, egg, gothic, catenary, semi-elliptical, basket handle, modified basket handle, or custom shapes. Has Manning's n, inlet/outlet offsets (pipe doesn't have to connect at the invert), and separate entry/exit loss coefficients.

EPANET Pipe: Always circular. Always full. Properties are length, diameter, roughness (C, ε, or n depending on formula choice), a single minor loss coefficient K, and a status (Open, Closed, or Check Valve). That's it — no shape selection, no offsets, no separate entry/exit losses.

The simplification: Because EPANET pipes are always full circles, there's no need for the [XSECTIONS] section at all. The geometry is defined entirely by the diameter in [PIPES]. If you're converting a SWMM model to EPANET (rare, but possible for force mains), you'd use only the pipe diameter and ignore all the shape complexity.

SWMM Pump: Uses a pump curve (head vs. flow or volume vs. flow). Controlled by on/off depths at upstream node, startup/shutoff depths, or rule-based controls. Four types: Type 1 (volume-depth), Type 2 (flow-depth), Type 3 (head-flow), Type 4 (variable speed). No energy cost tracking.

EPANET Pump: Uses a head-flow pump curve (like SWMM Type 3) or a power curve. Has speed settings (0.0 to 2.0, where 1.0 = rated speed) that shift the pump curve using affinity laws. Controlled by simple controls, rule-based controls, or time patterns.

What's new — Energy Analysis: EPANET computes energy consumption and costs for each pump: kWh used, average efficiency, peak demand charges, and cost per day. You define energy prices, efficiency curves, and price patterns in [ENERGY]. This is critical for water utility operations — pumping energy is typically 80%+ of a utility's operating cost. SWMM has no equivalent; it focuses on hydraulic capacity, not operational cost.

💡 Aha Moment

Demands are the "inflows" — but they leave, not enter

In SWMM you think about water entering the system (rainfall → subcatchment → junction inflow). In EPANET, water leaves at junctions as "demands" — customers consuming water. The source (reservoir) pushes water in; demands pull it out. It's the opposite direction of thinking, even though the math is the same conservation of mass.

⚠️ What Has No Equivalent

Five SWMM5 concepts that simply don’t exist in EPANET

Subcatchment — No hydrology layer. You must calculate demands externally (billing data, population, per-capita rates).

Rain Gage — No rainfall. Instead, demand patterns create time variation (morning/evening peaks, weekend shifts, seasonal factors).

Outfall — No downstream boundary. EPANET’s closest analog is a reservoir, but reservoirs are sources (fixed-head supply), not sinks. Water leaves via demands, not outfalls.

Divider — No flow-splitting rules. In EPANET, flow divides naturally by the pressure gradient through looped network topology.

Pipe cross-sections — No shapes (egg, horseshoe, basket handle). EPANET pipes are circular, always full. The [XSECTIONS] section does not exist.

The simplification is intentional — pressurized distribution networks don’t need these concepts because the physics is fundamentally different.

Demands: The Heart of EPANET Modeling

In SWMM, water enters the system through rainfall and inflows. In EPANET, water leaves the system at junctions as "demands." Understanding demands is like understanding subcatchments in SWMM — it's where the real-world hydrology meets the model.

SWMM5 — How Water Enters

Multiple inflow mechanisms

Water enters a SWMM model through many paths, each with its own section and parameters:

  • Subcatchment runoff — rainfall × area × runoff coefficient, routed to a node
  • Dry Weather Flow (DWF) — base sanitary flow at a node, with time-of-day multipliers
  • RDII (RTK) — rainfall-dependent infiltration/inflow via unit hydrographs
  • Direct inflows — user-defined time series at a node
  • Groundwater — lateral inflow from aquifer to node

All of these add water to the network. The system conveys it downstream to the outfall.

EPANET — How Water Leaves

Demands at junctions

Water is consumed at every junction through demands:

  • Base Demand — a constant withdrawal rate (GPM, LPS) at a node, like DWF's average value
  • Demand Pattern — time-varying multipliers on the base demand (diurnal curve), exactly like SWMM's DWF time patterns
  • Demand Categories — multiple demand types at one node (residential + commercial + fire flow), each with its own pattern
  • Emitters — pressure-dependent outflow at a node (Q = C·Pγ), used for sprinklers, leaks, and fire hydrants

All of these remove water from the network. Sources (reservoirs/tanks) provide it.

🔑 The Rosetta Stone for Demands

SWMM DWF ≈ EPANET Base Demand + Pattern

Your SWMM Dry Weather Flow at a junction — say 0.5 CFS with a diurnal pattern giving a morning peak at 8 AM and an evening peak at 6 PM — maps directly to an EPANET base demand with a demand pattern. The concept is identical: a base flow rate multiplied by a time-varying pattern. The only difference is direction (SWMM inflow vs. EPANET withdrawal) and units (CFS vs. GPM).

💡 Aha Moment

The patterns are literally the same format

SWMM's HOURLY DWF pattern and EPANET's demand pattern use the exact same syntax — a named pattern with 24 hourly multipliers. If you've built diurnal patterns for sanitary flow in SWMM, you already know how to create demand patterns in EPANET. The multiplier at hour H is applied to the base demand just like it's applied to the average DWF.

In SWMM, you can assign multiple DWF components to one node (FLOW + constituent loads). EPANET goes further with demand categories: a single junction can have multiple base demands, each with its own pattern.

For example, a junction serving a mixed-use area might have:

Category 1: Residential — 100 GPM, residential pattern (morning/evening peaks)

Category 2: Commercial — 50 GPM, commercial pattern (9-5 weekday peak)

Category 3: Fire flow — 0 GPM base, but available as a surge demand for fire flow analysis

This is defined in the [DEMANDS] section, separate from the default demand in [JUNCTIONS]. There's no exact SWMM equivalent — it's like having multiple DWF entries at one node, each with a different pattern.

EPANET emitters are pressure-dependent outflows: Q = C · Pγ, where C is the emitter coefficient, P is pressure at the node, and γ is the pressure exponent (default 0.5, giving orifice-type flow).

In SWMM terms, this is most like an orifice discharging to the atmosphere — flow depends on the head above the orifice. Uses include modeling leaky pipes (background leakage proportional to pressure), fire hydrant flow analysis, sprinkler irrigation systems, and calibrating unaccounted-for water losses.

There's no direct SWMM equivalent because SWMM junctions don't have pressure-dependent outflows — but if you've modeled an orifice at a storage node that discharges based on head, the concept is very similar.

Demand-driven (DDA) — traditional EPANET: demands are always fully satisfied regardless of available pressure. Even if pressure goes negative, the full demand is withdrawn. This is like SWMM always accepting all inflows at a junction even when it's flooding.

Pressure-driven (PDA) — EPANET 2.2+: demands are partially satisfied based on available pressure using the Wagner equation:

D = Df × [(P − Pmin) / (Preq − Pmin)]Pexp    for Pmin < P < Preq
Df = full demand  |  P = available pressure  |  Pmin = minimum pressure (zero demand below this, default 0 psi)  |  Preq = required pressure (full demand above this, default 0.1 psi)  |  Pexp = pressure exponent (default 0.5).
Set these via MINIMUM PRESSURE, REQUIRED PRESSURE, and PRESSURE EXPONENT in the [OPTIONS] section. Typical values: Pmin = 0–5 psi, Preq = 25–40 psi, Pexp = 0.5.

PDA is much more realistic for low-pressure scenarios (pipe breaks, fire flow, pump failures). It’s analogous to how SWMM’s surcharge algorithm limits inflow when a junction can’t accept more water — the system “pushes back.” If you’re modeling emergency scenarios in EPANET, always use PDA.

Hydraulic Equations: What Changes

You know Manning's equation cold. EPANET's default is Hazen-Williams — but the energy concepts you know from SWMM carry over directly.

SWMM5 — Manning's

Friction loss in open channels

Manning's Equation (US)
Q = (1.49/n) · A · R2/3 · S1/2

Where n = Manning's roughness (0.01–0.03 typical), A = flow area, R = hydraulic radius, S = friction slope. You've memorized that n=0.013 for new concrete pipe.

EPANET — Hazen-Williams

Friction loss in pressurized pipes

Hazen-Williams Headloss
hf = 4.727 · L · Q1.852 / (C1.852 · d4.87)
US form: Q in ft³/s, d in ft, L in ft, hf in ft  |  SI form uses constant 10.67 with Q in m³/s, d in m

Where C = Hazen-Williams coefficient (higher = smoother, opposite of Manning's n!). C=150 for new pipe, C=100 for old corroded pipe. EPANET also supports Darcy-Weisbach and Chezy-Manning as options.

⚠️ Watch Out

Roughness direction is inverted!

In Manning's, a higher n means rougher (more friction). In Hazen-Williams, a higher C means smoother (less friction). This trips up everyone. Think: Manning n = "nastiness" (high = bad), H-W C = "capacity" (high = good).

In SWMM's dynamic wave solver, you're solving the full Saint-Venant equations — continuity AND momentum in one dimension. This handles surcharging, backwater, reverse flow, and pressurized conditions.

EPANET uses a simpler but elegant formulation: at each node, conservation of mass (flow in = flow out + demand). Along each pipe, energy equation (head loss = headloss from friction + minor losses). These are solved simultaneously using the Gradient Algorithm.

The key simplification: because pipes are always full, there's no free surface to track, no wave propagation, no supercritical/subcritical transition. The trade-off is you can't model partially-full conditions — but for a pressurized distribution system, you never need to.

Both SWMM and EPANET handle minor (local) losses the same way:

hminor = K · V² / 2g

In SWMM, you enter K as an entrance/exit loss coefficient on a conduit. In EPANET, you enter K as a minor loss coefficient on a pipe. Same equation, same K values, same idea.

EPANET gives you three headloss options in [OPTIONS]:

1. Hazen-Williams (default, most common in US practice) — empirical, only valid for water at normal temperatures. Roughness = C coefficient.

2. Darcy-Weisbach (theoretically correct — the only headloss formula derived from dimensional analysis and the Navier-Stokes equations, not an empirical curve fit) — uses friction factor from the Moody diagram via Colebrook-White. Roughness = ε (pipe wall roughness height in mm or ft). Unlike H-W, D-W is valid for any fluid, any temperature, any pipe size, and any Reynolds number. H-W and Manning are empirical fits valid over limited ranges of Re, diameter (~3″ to ~72″ for H-W), and temperature (~60°F water). For anything outside those ranges, D-W is the only defensible choice.

3. Chezy-Manning — yes, your old friend! Uses Manning's n. EPANET applies it to full-pipe flow. So if you really want, you can use Manning's n in EPANET and feel right at home.

Why does H-W persist if D-W is theoretically superior?

The answer is largely historical. Allen Hazen and Gardner Williams published their formula in 1902 — decades before Lewis Moody's 1944 diagram made friction factor calculation practical. In an era of slide rules and nomographs, H-W's direct relationship between C, diameter, and headloss was a major computational convenience. That convenience became institutionalized: textbooks taught it, agencies specified it, databases were built around C values, and regulatory frameworks encoded it into design standards.

Engineers do not cling to H-W out of ignorance. They use it because it works well enough for typical municipal water at ambient temperatures in common pipe sizes (3″–72″), and because their entire professional ecosystem — calibrated models, utility records, design manuals — is built around C values. For most drinking water distribution work, H-W's limitations rarely matter. But when they do matter (non-ambient temperature, unusual fluids, extreme pipe sizes, laminar or transitional flow), there is no workaround — D-W is the only defensible choice.

The Solver: From Saint-Venant to Gradient Algorithm

SWMM's dynamic wave solver is one of the most complex in hydraulic modeling. Let's give it the attention it deserves before showing how EPANET's solver takes a fundamentally different approach.

📐 SWMM5's Engine Room

The Full Saint-Venant Equations — What You've Been Solving All Along

SWMM5's dynamic wave routing solves the one-dimensional Saint-Venant equations, derived from the Navier-Stokes equations integrated over the cross-section. These are two coupled partial differential equations — one for mass, one for momentum — that describe unsteady, non-uniform flow in open channels and closed conduits.

1. Continuity Equation (Conservation of Mass)
∂A/∂t + ∂Q/∂x = qlat
A = cross-sectional flow area (varies with depth and position)
Q = volumetric flow rate
qlat = lateral inflow per unit length (from subcatchments, DWF, RDII, groundwater)
t = time, x = distance along the conduit
In plain English: the rate of change of stored water in a conduit segment equals the net flow in/out plus any lateral inflows.
2. Momentum Equation (Conservation of Momentum)
∂Q/∂t + ∂(Q²/A)/∂x + gA·∂y/∂x = gA·(S₀ − Sf) − gA·hL
∂Q/∂t → Local acceleration: how flow changes over time at a fixed point
∂(Q²/A)/∂x → Convective acceleration: how momentum changes along the pipe as velocity and area vary
gA·∂y/∂x → Pressure gradient: force from water depth variation along the conduit
gA·S₀ → Gravity: bed slope driving the flow (S₀ = −∂z₀/∂x)
gA·Sf → Friction force: resistance from Manning's equation, Sf = (n·Q / (1.49·A·R2/3))²
gA·hL → Local losses: entrance, exit, and junction losses (K·V²/2g form)
Convention note: This uses the SWMM5 Reference Manual (Vol. II) convention with depth y and bed slope S₀ separated. The equivalent compact form using total head H (where H = z₀ + y, so ∂H/∂x = ∂y/∂x − S₀) is: ∂Q/∂t + ∂(Q²/A)/∂x + gA·∂H/∂x + gA·Sf + gA·hL = 0. Both forms are identical; this document uses the y-based form throughout.

SWMM5 uses a staggered finite-difference scheme on a space-time grid. Here's the approach you've been relying on for decades:

Spatial discretization: Each conduit is treated as a single computational element (not subdivided into multiple cells like HEC-RAS). Continuity is applied at nodes (junctions), and momentum is applied along links (conduits). This node-link staggering is the key to SWMM's efficiency — it avoids solving for conditions at internal conduit points.

Temporal discretization: An explicit time-marching scheme advances the solution forward. At each timestep:

Step 1: Solve the momentum equation for each link to get the new flow Q at time t+Δt, using heads from the upstream and downstream nodes at time t.

Step 2: Solve the continuity equation at each node to get the new head H at time t+Δt, by summing all flows entering and leaving the node plus any external inflows.

Step 3: Update all node depths and link flows. Check for surcharging — if a node's head exceeds the crown of all connected conduits, switch to the surcharge algorithm. Check for flooding — if head exceeds the ground elevation (maximum depth), excess water is either ponded or lost.

The surcharge algorithm: When pipes pressurize, the free surface vanishes and the standard Saint-Venant equations no longer apply. SWMM5 uses a modified approach: the continuity equation at a surcharged node is reformulated to account for the node's zero surface area (no change in stored volume, only throughflow). The Preissmann slot concept — an imaginary narrow slot at the top of a closed conduit — allows a smooth transition between free-surface and pressurized flow, maintaining a virtual free surface even in surcharged conditions.

The 1928 paper by Courant, Friedrichs, and Lewy established the fundamental stability condition for finite difference methods — and it governs every timestep SWMM5 takes. Because SWMM5 uses an explicit scheme, the timestep is limited by the CFL condition:

Δt ≤ Δx / (V + c)

Where Δx is the conduit length, V is the flow velocity, and c = √(gA/T) is the gravity wave celerity (T = top width). For a typical 400-ft pipe with 5 ft/s flow and a wave celerity of 15 ft/s, the maximum stable timestep is about 20 seconds.

Why this matters: Short pipes with fast flow require very small timesteps. A 50-ft pipe with a 10 ft/s velocity and 20 ft/s celerity gives a maximum Δt of about 1.7 seconds. This is why SWMM models with short pipes and steep slopes can be painfully slow — and why you've learned to avoid very short conduits or to use the "lengthening" option.

SWMM's variable timestep: SWMM5 can automatically adjust the timestep to satisfy the Courant condition across all conduits. It computes the CFL for each link and uses the smallest value (multiplied by a safety factor from the [OPTIONS] variable time step). This is why your dynamic wave simulations might show Δt oscillating between 0.5 and 30 seconds — SWMM is constantly adapting.

What happens when Courant is violated: Numerical oscillations, non-physical flow reversals, mass balance errors, and the dreaded continuity error climbing above 1%. If you've ever seen flow oscillating wildly between positive and negative values at a junction, the Courant condition was likely violated.

SWMM5 offers three routing methods of increasing complexity. Understanding what's "missing" in each helps you appreciate what EPANET's steady-state approach gives up:

1. Steady Flow: Translates inflow hydrographs through conduits with no delay, attenuation, or storage. Qout(t) = Qin(t). No momentum, no continuity storage. This is closest to how EPANET thinks — instantaneous propagation.

2. Kinematic Wave: Solves continuity + a simplified momentum equation where the friction slope Sf equals the bed slope S0. This means: no backwater effects, no reverse flow, no pressurized flow, no looped networks. Flow can only go downstream. It does capture hydrograph translation and attenuation. Timesteps can be longer (minutes).

3. Dynamic Wave (Full Saint-Venant): Solves both continuity and full momentum including local acceleration, convective acceleration, pressure gradient, and friction. Handles everything: backwater, reverse flow, surcharging, looped networks, tidal outfalls, storage, weirs, orifices, pumps. This is what most SWMM modelers use for any serious analysis — and what EPANET doesn't need because the physics of pressurized flow are inherently simpler.

The irony: EPANET's pressurized pipe networks are topologically more complex (heavily looped) but hydraulically simpler (no free surface, no wave propagation, no surcharge transitions). SWMM's tree-like collection networks are topologically simpler but hydraulically richer.

At every SWMM junction, the continuity equation is:

As · dH/dt = ΣQin - ΣQout + Qexternal

Where As is the surface area of water at the junction (from the cross-section geometry or from a ponded area). This is what determines the rate of rise and fall of water at a node.

The surface area issue: In SWMM, a junction's surface area is tiny (the manhole shaft) when the water level is in the pipe, but can be large if ponded area is specified. This small surface area means that small flow imbalances cause rapid head changes — which is why SWMM junctions can be "twitchy" and why the solver needs such small timesteps near surcharged conditions.

In EPANET: There is no surface area at junctions (they have zero storage). The continuity equation becomes simply: ΣQin = ΣQout + Demand. No dH/dt term — the system is always in equilibrium. This is the fundamental simplification that makes EPANET's solver so stable.

When a closed conduit surcharges in SWMM, the free surface disappears — and Saint-Venant equations require a free surface to define the gravity wave celerity c = √(gA/T). The Preissmann slot is an elegant numerical trick:

Imagine an infinitely tall, very narrow slot at the top of the pipe. The width of the slot is chosen so that the wave speed in the slot matches the pressure wave speed in a full pipe (typically ~1000 ft/s). This allows the same Saint-Venant equations to smoothly transition from free-surface to pressurized flow without changing the solver.

The slot width is calculated as: Tslot = gAfull / c² where c is the target pressure wave celerity. For a 48-inch pipe with c = 1000 ft/s, the slot width is about 0.04 ft — very narrow.

Why this matters for understanding EPANET: SWMM's Preissmann slot is essentially a trick to make gravity-flow equations work for pressurized conditions. EPANET doesn't need this trick because it starts from pressurized-flow assumptions. EPANET pipes are "all slot, no pipe" — the entire cross section is always under pressure.

Now Compare: EPANET's Gradient Algorithm

SWMM5 — Dynamic Wave Summary

Transient, explicit, wave-tracking

Two coupled PDEs (continuity + momentum). Explicit time-marching, Courant-limited timesteps (1–30 sec). Handles free-surface, surcharging, backwater, reverse flow. Node-link staggered grid with Preissmann slot for pressurized transition.

Unknowns at each timestep: Flow in every link + Head at every node

EPANET — Global Gradient Algorithm

Steady-state, iterative, equilibrium-finding

At each timestep, solves for hydraulic equilibrium using the "Global Gradient Algorithm" (GGA). The theoretical foundation was laid by Todini and Pilati (1988), but EPANET’s implementation is based on Rossman’s 2000 reformulation, which exploits the sparse network structure for computational efficiency — the algorithm in EPANET is not a direct implementation of the 1988 paper.

  • Mass balance at every node: ΣQin = ΣQout + Demand
  • Energy along every pipe: Hi - Hj = hf(Q) + hm(Q)
  • Linearize the nonlinear headloss equations (Newton-Raphson)
  • Solve the sparse linear system (Ax = b)
  • Iterate until flow/head changes are below tolerance

Unknowns at each timestep: Same — Flow in every link + Head at every node. But solved as a single simultaneous system, not marched through time.

Timestep: typically 1 hour (hydraulic), 5 min (quality). Unconditionally stable.

🔑 Key Concept

Extended Period Simulation (EPS) ≈ SWMM's Quasi-Steady

EPANET doesn't track wave propagation through pipes. Instead, it solves a steady-state at each timestep, then updates tank levels and demand patterns for the next step. It's like running SWMM in steady-state mode, then jumping forward in time, updating boundary conditions, and running steady-state again. This "Extended Period Simulation" (EPS) is how you model a 24-hour or 7-day operational scenario — tank filling/draining cycles, pump on/off, demand variations. The key equation for tank updating between steps is:

Vtank(t+Δt) = Vtank(t) + (ΣQin - ΣQout) · Δt
⚠️ Critical Distinction

EPS is NOT Transient — Don’t Be Fooled by the Timesteps

Extended Period Simulation (EPS) looks like dynamic simulation because it marches through time with timesteps. But it is fundamentally different from SWMM5’s transient solver:

SWMM5 Dynamic Wave
  • Each timestep continues from the previous one
  • Waves propagate through the network
  • Inertia and acceleration terms are tracked
  • Pressure changes travel at finite celerity
  • History matters — the solution remembers its past
EPANET EPS
  • Each timestep is a fresh steady-state solve
  • No wave propagation at all
  • No inertia — purely algebraic equations
  • Pressure changes affect the entire network instantly
  • Only tank levels and control states carry forward

Practical implication: If you close a valve at timestep t in EPANET, the pressure impact reaches every node at timestep t — not t+1, t+2, etc. as the pressure wave travels. There is no water hammer, no surge, no transient oscillation. For those phenomena, you need a dedicated transient solver (such as Bentley HAMMER, AFT Impulse, or InfoSurge).

No Courant condition. No instabilities. No tiny timesteps.

You know SWMM's dynamic wave solver can blow up with a bad Courant number. EPANET's solver is inherently stable because it finds equilibrium at each step — there's no wave propagation to track. A 1-hour hydraulic timestep is perfectly fine for most analyses. The Gradient Algorithm typically converges in 5–10 iterations per timestep, even for networks with thousands of pipes. Contrast that with SWMM needing to march through 3,600 one-second timesteps to cover a single EPANET hour.

The Global Gradient Algorithm: Why Loops Demand It

In a tree network, you can solve pipe-by-pipe from upstream to downstream — each pipe’s flow is known from mass balance. In a looped network, multiple paths exist between source and demand, and you must solve for all flows simultaneously. This is what the Global Gradient Algorithm does (Todini & Pilati 1988, with EPANET’s implementation based on Rossman’s 2000 sparse-matrix reformulation).

For each pipe connecting nodes i and j:
  Q_ij = f(H_i - H_j)    [flow is a function of head difference]

For each node i:
  Σ_j Q_ij = Demand_i     [mass balance]

Substitute the first into the second:
  Σ_j f(H_i - H_j) = Demand_i    [N equations in N unknowns: H₁...Hₙ]

Linearize f(ΔH) ≈ f(ΔH⁰) + f'(ΔH⁰)·(ΔH - ΔH⁰):
  This gives: [A]{ΔH} = {b}   where A is a sparse N×N matrix

The sparsity pattern of A mirrors the network topology:
  A_ii ≠ 0 for all i (diagonal — always present)
  A_ij ≠ 0 only if nodes i and j are connected by a pipe

In a LOOPED network, each node connects to 3-6 others →
  A has 3-6 non-zeros per row → SPARSE matrix

Solve using sparse Cholesky factorization →
  O(N^1.1 to N^1.3) operations
  MUCH faster than dense matrix solution O(N³)

This is why EPANET can solve 100,000+ node networks in seconds. The sparsity is a gift from the network topology: most nodes connect to only 3–6 neighbors.

Water Quality: From Pollutographs to Chlorine Decay

SWMM tracks multiple pollutants through a collection system. EPANET tracks one constituent through a distribution system — but does it with remarkable sophistication.

EPANET — Water Quality

Single constituent, operational focus

  • One substance per simulation

  • Contaminant propagation tracking
  • Water age tracking (built-in)
  • Source tracing (built-in, one-click)

The primary water quality use case in EPANET is modeling chlorine residual through a distribution system. Chlorine decays by two mechanisms:

Bulk decay: Reactions in the water volume itself. First-order: C(t) = C₀ · e-kb·t where kb is the bulk decay coefficient.

Wall decay: Reactions at the pipe wall (biofilm, pipe material). Depends on mass transfer and wall reaction rate kw. This is where pipe age and material matter — old cast iron pipes consume chlorine much faster than new PVC.

If you've modeled pollutant decay in SWMM using first-order kinetics, the bulk decay is conceptually identical. The wall reaction is unique to EPANET and has no SWMM equivalent.

EPANET can compute the age of water at every node — how long since it entered the system from a reservoir. This is analogous to tracking travel time in SWMM, but in EPANET it's a built-in analysis type (no pollutant definition needed).

Water age is critical for identifying dead ends, oversized mains, and areas where water quality degrades from long residence times.

EPANET can trace what percentage of water at any node came from a specific source. If you have two treatment plants feeding a network, you can see the blending zones. EPANET has this as a dedicated, built-in analysis type — just select "Trace" and pick a source node.

You can do this in SWMM5 too — by defining a conservative pollutant (zero decay coefficient) and assigning a concentration of 100 at the source node inflow and 0 everywhere else. The resulting concentration at any downstream node tells you the percentage of water from that source. It's the same advection-mixing math, just without the convenience of a one-click "Trace" mode. For multiple sources, you'd define multiple conservative pollutants — one per source — which SWMM handles natively since it supports multi-pollutant simulations.

So the difference isn't capability — it's convenience. EPANET makes source tracing a first-class feature. In SWMM, you roll your own with the pollutant engine you already know.

EPANET Leakage Workflow
  1. Determine total system leakage (water balance or minimum night flow). Typical NRW: 15–30%
  2. Distribute to nodes: proportional to pipe length, service connections, or uniform
  3. Calculate Ce = Qleak / Pavgγ at each node
  4. Calibrate: run model, compare pressures to field data, adjust Ce
  5. Pressure management: reduce PRV setpoint → P drops → leakage drops

  2. Separate: GWI (constant baseflow) vs RDII (rain-dependent)
  3. For exfiltration: compare upstream/downstream monitors. If downstream < upstream → exfiltration

  5. Alternative: storage node seepage for known locations (leaking manholes, basins)

Gotchas

For SWMM5 Experts Moving to EPANET
#1: No groundwater table. EPANET assumes exterior = atmospheric pressure. You cannot model a rising water table reducing leakage.
#2: Emitters don’t distinguish pipe material. Ce is at the node, not the pipe. FAVAD helps by putting coefficients on pipes, but adoption is limited.
#3: Leakage is always a loss. In SWMM5, infiltration adds flow. In EPANET, leakage removes flow. Same defect, opposite mass balance effect.
#4: No time-varying soil response. SWMM5’s Green-Ampt captures the wetting front. EPANET emitters give constant Q for a given P — no memory of soil moisture.
For EPANET Experts Moving to SWMM5
#1: No pipe-level exfiltration. SWMM5 conduits have no equivalent of FAVAD. You must use storage node seepage or the GW module as workarounds.
#2: The GW equation is not intuitive. EPANET’s Q=C·Pγ is simple. SWMM5’s five-coefficient three-term equation is powerful but opaque. The interaction term (A3·Dgw·Dsw) has no EPANET analog.
#3: Exfiltration can cause dry pipes. In EPANET, a leak reduces pressure but the pipe stays full. In SWMM5, exfiltration can empty a pipe — the solver behavior changes completely.
#4: I/I calibration is much harder. EPANET uses minimum night flow (one number per DMA). SWMM5 requires separating baseflow infiltration from rain-dependent I/I across multiple wet/dry periods.

The Asymmetry Explained

EPANET sees only one direction

The emitter equation Q = C×Pγ takes internal pressure P as input. When P>0, water flows out. When P≤0, flow is zero. There is no mechanism for water to flow into the pipe. The curve lives entirely in the positive quadrant.

Cannot model: contamination intrusion during depressurization events.

SWMM5 sees both directions

The GW equation computes flow from the head difference. When GW is higher → infiltration. When pipe level is higher → exfiltration. The same pipe can be an infiltration source in March and an exfiltration source in August.

Captures: seasonal I/I reversal from first principles in a single calibrated model.

Infiltration is Expensive
  • Larger pipes needed
  • Higher pump station capacity
  • More water to treat at WWTP
  • Increased CSO/SSO risk

Exfiltration is a Contamination Risk
  • Untreated sewage entering soil/GW

  • Nutrient loading to streams
  • Harder to detect (reduces downstream flow)
  • Often occurs in dry weather when monitoring is less common
Leakage is Lost Revenue
  • Treated, pumped water never billed
  • Treatment chemicals & energy wasted
  • Reduced system pressure at peak demand
  • Global: 30–40% lost in many systems
  • US: 16% average (AWWA 2022). At $2–5/kgal, 2 MGD lost = $1.5–3.6M/yr
The unified view: Same pipe defect. Same Darcy’s law. Different operating pressure. EPANET’s pressurized mains always push water out (ΔH always positive), so it only needs a one-directional model. SWMM5’s gravity sewers fluctuate above and below the GW table, so it needs a bidirectional model. The physics is identical — the operating regime is what differs.
The real-world perspective: EPANET typically models water leaving pipes (leakage) and SWMM5 models water entering pipes (infiltration). Exfiltration from sewers is relatively rare and hard to detect — the overlooked sibling of infiltration. But when it matters (contaminated sewage exfiltrating into groundwater near a wellfield), it really matters. And SWMM5’s groundwater module is the only one of these tools that can model it from first principles.
Operating Regimes: Why the Models Are Fundamentally Different

The operating regime — pressurized vs. gravity — determines which direction water can flow through defects, how much driving force exists, and what mathematical model is appropriate. This is a physics constraint that dictates the software design.

Regime 1: Pressurized Distribution (EPANET)

Internal pressure: 40–100 psi (90–230 ft head). External head: 5–20 ft (groundwater). ΔH is almost always positive and large:

Normal operation: ΔH = +235 ft → OUTWARD
Fire flow demand: ΔH = +165 ft → STILL outward
Pump failure: ΔH = +105 ft → STILL outward
Main break nearby: ΔH = +35 ft → STILL outward
Full depressurization: ΔH = 0 → crossover
Below this: ΔH < 0 → intrusion (EPANET can’t model)

Ignoring external head (7 psi GW vs 60 psi internal) introduces ~6% error — acceptable for engineering practice. This is why the emitter has no term for external head.

Regime 2: Gravity Sewer (SWMM5)

Pipe water depth: 0 to surcharge. GW table fluctuates seasonally. ΔH changes sign multiple times per year:

Spring (wet): pipe 3ft, GW 8ft → ΔH = −5 ft INWARD
Summer (dry): pipe 2ft, GW 1ft → ΔH = +1 ft OUTWARD
Storm (surcharge): pipe 12ft, GW 6ft → ΔH = +6 ft OUT
Post-storm: pipe 4ft, GW 9ft → ΔH = −5 ft INWARD

The head difference changes sign multiple times per year — and sometimes multiple times per storm event. A one-directional model cannot capture this.

How Darcy’s Law Connects Everything

Darcy’s law (1856) governs flow through porous media: Q = −K × A × (dh/dl). Flow rate is proportional to the pressure difference and the material’s permeability, inversely proportional to distance. It is the foundation for all pipe leakage and seepage modeling.

Two Resistances in Series

When water crosses a pipe wall through a defect, it passes through two resistances: the defect itself (orifice) and the surrounding soil (Darcy). The overall flow rate is governed by whichever is larger:

Defect-Controlled (γ ≈ 0.5)

Small crack in rigid pipe, sandy soil. The crack is the bottleneck — soil can accept all the water. Flow follows the orifice equation Q ∝ √P.

Soil-Controlled (γ ≈ 1.0)

Large joint separation, clay soil. The joint is wide open — clay limits seepage rate. Flow follows Darcy’s law Q ∝ P (linear).

Both Matter (0.5 < γ < 1.0)

Most real situations. The UK water industry uses γ≈1.18 as a national average. Variable-area leaks (γ≈1.5) add defect expansion under pressure.

Infiltration vs. Exfiltration: A Seasonal Story

Infiltration is water entering a pipe from outside through defects. Exfiltration is water leaving a pipe to the outside through the same defects. Same physics, opposite directions — determined entirely by which side has higher hydraulic head.

One Crack, Four Seasons

Consider a single cracked joint in a 12-inch vitrified clay sewer pipe buried 8 feet deep:

{
    double p1 = Node[Link[k].N1].head - Node[Link[k].N1].el;
    double p2 = Node[Link[k].N2].head - Node[Link[k].N2].el;
    double pavg = (p1 + p2) / 2.0;

SWMM5 — Groundwater Bidirectional (gwater.c)

double getGWFlow(int j, double gwHead, double nodeHead)
{
    double dgw = gwHead - Aquifer[j].bottomElev;
    double dsw = nodeHead - Aquifer[j].nodeElev;
    double qgw = GW[j].a1 * pow(MAX(0, dgw), GW[j].b1);  // infiltration +
    qgw -= GW[j].a2 * pow(MAX(0, dsw), GW[j].b2);         // exfiltration -
    qgw += GW[j].a3 * dgw * dsw;                           // interaction
    return qgw;  // positive = into pipe, negative = out of pipe
}

Here's what SWMM5 actually does in forcemain.c to convert Hazen-Williams C to an equivalent Manning's n for force main display and initialization:

// From SWMM5 forcemain.c — forcemain_getEquivN() // Computes equivalent Manning's n from Hazen-Williams C double forcemain_getEquivN(int j, int k) { double a1, a2, r; // Manning constant: 1.486 (US) or 1.0 (SI) a1 = PHI; // Manning's constant // H-W constant: 1.318 (US) or 0.8492 (SI) a2 = Cf; // Hazen-Williams constant // Hydraulic radius at full depth r = xsect_getRofY(&Link[j].xsect, 1.0); // n = (a1/a2) × R^(2/3 - 0.63) / C // The exponent difference (0.0367) makes n // weakly dependent on pipe diameter return (a1 / a2) * pow(r, 2./3. - 0.63) / Conduit[k].roughness; // roughness = C for H-W }

Key insight: SWMM drops the friction slope term (Sf0.04) from the full derivation, treating it as ≈ 1.0. This makes the conversion independent of flow conditions but introduces a 20–40% error in the friction slope when you compare Manning's equation using this equivalent n against the original H-W equation. It doesn't matter for simulation accuracy because SWMM uses H-W directly during force main routing — the equivalent n is only for display.

The full derivation: Set Manning velocity = H-W velocity and solve for n:

// Manning: V = (φ/n) × R^(2/3) × Sf^(1/2) // H-W: V = Cf × C × R^(0.63) × Sf^(0.54) // // Set equal, solve for n: // n = (φ / (Cf × C)) × R^(2/3 - 0.63) × Sf^(1/2 - 0.54) // n = (φ / (Cf × C)) × R^(0.0367) × Sf^(-0.04) // ↑ SWMM drops this // Because Sf^(-0.04) ≈ 1.0 for typical slopes // At Sf = 0.001: Sf^(-0.04) = 1.32 (32% error) // At Sf = 0.01: Sf^(-0.04) = 1.10 (10% error) // At Sf = 0.1: Sf^(-0.04) = 1.04 ( 4% error)

How the Force Main Conversion Works

1. The Problem

SWMM5 uses Manning's equation for partial flow. For force mains (full flow), you choose Hazen-Williams or Darcy-Weisbach. SWMM needs a single n for consistency when the pipe transitions between regimes.

2. The Solution

SWMM equates full-pipe Manning flow to H-W (or D-W) flow at the same slope, solving for the equivalent n that produces identical head loss. For H-W, this depends on diameter and slope. For D-W, only on diameter and roughness height.

3. The Caveat

The equivalent n is approximate because Manning's and H-W use different exponents. SWMM uses H-W directly during full-flow routing — the equivalent n is only for display and the transition zone near surcharge.

When Does the n↔C Conversion Actually Matter?

SWMM5 switches equations based on whether the force main is full or partially full. The equivalent n ensures continuity at the transition.

💡 The Key Question

Does your force main ever run partially full?

If the pump runs continuously and the main is always pressurized, the equivalent n is never used — SWMM applies H-W directly. The conversion only matters during pump startup, shutdown, and cycling when the main drains down and refills. For pump stations with frequent on/off cycling, the transition zone matters. For constant-speed pumps running 24/7, it doesn't.

⚠️ Low/Adverse Slope Warning

The formula has (D/S₀)^0.04 — as S₀ approaches zero, the equivalent n goes to infinity. Many force mains are laid flat or even go uphill (adverse slope). SWMM5 enforces an internal minimum slope to prevent this singularity.

At very low slopes, the Reference Manual formula (D/S₀-based) becomes unreliable, while the Source Code formula (R-based) is slope-independent and more stable. This is likely why SWMM5's actual source code uses the R-based form instead of the textbook D/S₀ form.

Gotchas for SWMM Veterans

The things that will trip you up, confuse you, or surprise you when you first open EPANET.

⚠️ Gotcha #1

Flow units change everything

SWMM typically uses CFS (cubic feet per second) or CMS. EPANET defaults to GPM (gallons per minute) in US units or LPS (liters per second) in SI. This affects how you read demands, pump curves, and results. The [OPTIONS] section's "Units" keyword sets this — and unlike SWMM, the flow unit choice also determines whether you're in US or SI for all other parameters.

⚠️ Gotcha #2

Elevation means something different

In SWMM, junction elevation = invert elevation (bottom of the pipe). In EPANET, junction elevation = the elevation of the node (ground or pipeline level). There's no "invert" concept because pipes are full. Pressure = (Total Head - Elevation). If you set an EPANET junction elevation to 0, you're saying it's at sea level — not at the pipe bottom.

⚠️ Gotcha #3

Negative pressures are your "flooding"

In SWMM, you worry about flooding (surcharge + ponding). In EPANET, the analogous problem is negative pressures — nodes where pressure drops below zero (or below the minimum service pressure). This means the system can't deliver water. It's the EPANET equivalent of "your system is failing."

⚠️ Gotcha #4

Hydraulic changes propagate instantly — but quality transport does not

In SWMM’s dynamic wave, flow propagates through the network at a finite speed governed by wave celerity. In EPANET’s hydraulic solver, changing a pump status or valve setting affects the entire network simultaneously at that timestep — there is no wave travel time.

However, EPANET absolutely does track finite transport time for water quality. The quality solver uses advective transport through pipes with a quality timestep (typically 5 minutes) specifically to capture travel time, mixing at junctions, and reaction kinetics along the flow path. A parcel of water entering a 10,000-ft main at 2 ft/s takes ~83 minutes to traverse it, and EPANET models this correctly. The distinction is: hydraulic equilibrium is instantaneous, constituent transport is time-dependent.

⚠️ Gotcha #5

Demand-driven vs pressure-driven analysis

Traditional EPANET assumes demands are always met regardless of pressure (demand-driven). This is like SWMM always accepting inflows at a junction regardless of capacity. Newer EPANET versions (2.2+) support pressure-driven demand where demand decreases with low pressure — much more realistic, like SWMM's surcharge limiting inflow.

⚠️ Gotcha #7

The roughness direction is reversed

Manning’s n and Hazen-Williams C measure roughness in opposite directions. This is the #1 numerical mistake when crossing between tools.

Manning’s n (SWMM5)
New CI pipe: n ≈ 0.012 (low = smooth)
Old corroded: n ≈ 0.025 (high = rough)
Higher n → rougher → more friction
Hazen-Williams C (EPANET)
New CI pipe: C ≈ 130 (high = smooth)
Old corroded: C ≈ 60 (low = rough)
Higher C → smoother → less friction

Rule of thumb: If your EPANET headloss seems wildly wrong, check whether you accidentally entered a Manning’s-style number (0.013) as a H-W coefficient. That would mean C=0.013 — an impossibly rough pipe that produces infinite headloss.

⚠️ Gotcha #8

Negative pressure is not “dry pipe” — it’s contamination risk

In SWMM5, you worry about flooding (water overtopping manholes). In EPANET, the mirror-image failure is negative pressure.

What negative pressure means physically: The hydraulic grade line drops below the node elevation. The pipe cannot deliver water at that location. In reality this creates a partial vacuum, which can cause:

  • Air entrainment — air enters through service connections and air/vacuum relief valves
  • Column separation — the water column breaks, leading to transient pressure spikes when it rejoins
  • Contamination intrusion — groundwater, soil bacteria, or surface contaminants are sucked INTO the pipe through joints and cracks
  • Service interruption — customers at that elevation get no water

SWMM5 analog: Negative pressure in EPANET is conceptually similar to flooding in SWMM5 — both mean the system has exceeded its capacity at that point. But the consequences are different: SWMM5 flooding is visible (water on the street); EPANET contamination intrusion is invisible and potentially deadly.

💡 The Good News

Your hydraulic intuition transfers 90%

Conservation of mass and energy. Head = elevation + pressure/depth. Friction increases with roughness, length, flow, and decreases with diameter. Pumps add energy, losses remove it. These fundamentals are identical. The other 10% is learning new vocabulary, different default units, and thinking "pressure" instead of "depth." Your 50 years of hydraulic intuition will serve you very well.

Which Tool? SWMM5 vs EPANET Decision Guide

The most common mistake isn't a bad translation — it's using the wrong tool entirely. This section helps you decide when to stay in SWMM5, when to move to EPANET, and when you need both.

Decision Flowchart

When NOT to Translate — Stay in SWMM5

🔵 Stay in SWMM5 for:
• Rainfall-runoff modeling — EPANET has zero hydrology. No subcatchments, no infiltration, no runoff.
• Combined sewer systems — EPANET can't model CSO diversions, regulators, or wet-weather overflow.
• Open-channel flow — EPANET is pressurized-only. No free surface, no supercritical/subcritical transitions.

• Water distribution operations — tank filling/draining cycles, pump scheduling, pressure zones.
• Fire flow analysis — can the system deliver 1,500 GPM at 20 psi residual at node X?
• Chlorine residual modeling — bulk + wall decay, maintaining minimum residual throughout the network.
• Water age analysis — identifying dead ends, oversized mains, stagnation zones.

Convergence Troubleshooting: SWMM5 vs EPANET

You're used to SWMM5's convergence behavior. Here's how EPANET's differs and what to do when things go wrong.

SWMM5 — Convergence Issues

What you're used to

Continuity error > 10% — Courant condition violated. Reduce timestep or lengthen short conduits.
Flow oscillations — Numerical instability at junctions with small surface area. Increase ponded area or reduce timestep.
"Node X flooded" — Head exceeds max depth. Increase max depth, add ponded area, or upsize pipe.
Negative depths — Usually a stability issue. Check for very steep slopes + small pipes + large timestep.
Pump cycling wildly — Control rules fighting each other. Add hysteresis (different on/off depths).
EPANET — Convergence Issues

What's different

"System unbalanced" — Gradient algorithm didn't converge within max trials. Increase TRIALS in [OPTIONS] (default 40, try 200).
Negative pressures — Node demand exceeds supply at available pressure. Check for disconnected nodes, closed pipes, or inadequate source head.
System disconnected — Nodes unreachable from any reservoir/tank. Check pipe status (Open/Closed) and valve settings.
Pump cannot deliver — Operating point off the pump curve. Check static head vs. pump shutoff head. Add a check valve.
Hydraulic equations unbalanced — Usually a modeling error: zero-length pipe, pump with no curve, valve in wrong orientation.

In SWMM5, you tune convergence with the routing timestep, Courant safety factor, and THREADS. In EPANET, the key settings are:

TRIALS — Maximum iterations per hydraulic timestep (default 40). If you get "unbalanced," try 200. If that doesn't help, it's a modeling error, not a solver issue.

ACCURACY — Convergence criterion for flow changes (default 0.01 = 1%). Tighter values (0.001) give more precision but need more iterations.

UNBALANCED CONTINUE 10 — If the solver can't converge, continue the simulation for up to 10 more trials instead of stopping. Useful for models with complex pump controls that occasionally have transient imbalances.

CHECKFREQ / MAXCHECK / DAMPLIMIT — Advanced convergence controls. CHECKFREQ sets how often the solver checks for status changes (valve/pump). DAMPLIMIT controls numerical damping. You rarely need these unless your model has many interacting control rules.

Key difference from SWMM5: EPANET's solver either converges (and the answer is correct) or it doesn't (and tells you). There's no gradual degradation like SWMM's rising continuity error. It's binary — works or doesn't.

Zero-demand nodes: Unlike SWMM5 where a junction with no inflow just sits there quietly, EPANET junctions with zero demand can cause issues in dead-end branches — no flow means no headloss calculation, which can make pressures undefined. Always give dead-end nodes a tiny demand (0.001 GPM) or connect them through a loop.

Elevation datum: SWMM5 uses invert elevation (bottom of pipe). EPANET uses ground/pipe elevation. If you're converting a SWMM5 model to EPANET, you need to shift all elevations from invert to ground — typically by adding the pipe diameter. Don't just copy the numbers.

Pump curves: SWMM5 accepts 1-point, 3-point, and 4-point pump curves with specific curve types (Type 1–4). EPANET accepts any number of points on a head-flow curve. If converting, you may need to resample SWMM's curve at more points, especially for variable-speed pumps where the affinity laws shift the entire curve.

Pipe status: EPANET pipes can be Open, Closed, or CV (check valve). SWMM5 conduits don't have a status field — they're always "open" and flow direction is determined by hydraulics. If you need a one-way pipe in EPANET, set the status to CV.

Transient Checklist: When EPANET's Steady-State Breaks Down

If you're a SWMM5 modeler accustomed to tracking wave propagation, here's when EPANET's steady-state assumption will mislead you:

⚠ Water hammer from rapid valve closure — EPANET won't show the pressure spike. Need a transient solver (HAMMER, AFT Impulse).
⚠ Pump trip (sudden power failure) — EPANET sees the pump off at the next timestep; it doesn't model the inertial rundown or pressure wave.
⚠ Rapid demand changes (fire flow startup) — EPANET applies the new demand instantly; no propagation delay through the network.
⚠ Column separation and vapor cavity collapse — These require transient modeling with cavitation. EPANET can't detect them.
✓ Slow operational changes (tank filling, demand patterns) — EPANET handles these perfectly with EPS. No transient solver needed.
✓ Pump scheduling with gradual transitions — If pumps ramp up/down over minutes, EPANET's timestep resolution is fine.
✓ Valve position changes over hours — PRV/PSV setpoint changes between timesteps are well-handled.
🔑 The Rule of Thumb

If it happens faster than your EPANET timestep, you need a different tool

EPANET's 1-hour hydraulic timestep means anything that changes on a seconds-to-minutes timescale is invisible. SWMM5's 1-second timestep captures events that happen within seconds. For water distribution, the question is: does the event happen faster than the EPS timestep? If yes, you need either SWMM5 (for free-surface transients) or a dedicated transient solver (for pressure-wave transients like water hammer).

EPANET-MSX vs SWMM5 Water Quality — The Full Picture

The Fundamental Distinction

They serve different domains and different problems. SWMM5 models collection systems (storm/sanitary sewers) with gravity-driven, partially full pipes — water enters via rainfall/runoff. EPANET-MSX models distribution systems (drinking water) with pressure-driven, always full pipes — water leaves via demand/consumption.

What EPANET-MSX Is

EPANET-MSX (Multi-Species eXtension) is an add-on to EPANET that allows modeling of multiple interacting chemical and biological species simultaneously. Standard EPANET can only model a single constituent per simulation (chlorine decay OR water age OR source tracing). MSX removes this limitation, enabling:

  • Auto-decomposition of chloramines to ammonia
  • Formation of disinfection byproducts (DBPs)
  • Biological regrowth and nitrification dynamics
  • Combined reaction rate constants in multi-source systems
  • Mass transfer limited oxidation and pipe wall adsorption reactions

How They’re Similar

Both solve the advection-reaction equation: Transport + Reaction = Concentration Change. Both track parcels of water as they move through pipes (Lagrangian approach), assume complete mixing at junctions, and allow user-defined reaction kinetics.

Key Concepts

      

        

          
Required Fire Flow

          
The flow needed for firefighting, typically 500–3,500 GPM depending on building size, construction type, and proximity to neighbors. Set by the local fire code or ISO guidelines.

        

        

          
Residual Pressure

          
The pressure remaining at the fire hydrant while delivering fire flow. Must stay above 20 psi (typical minimum). Below 20 psi risks collapsing the pipe, drawing in contamination, and losing supply to other customers.

        

        

          
Available Fire Flow

          
The maximum flow a hydrant can deliver while maintaining the minimum residual pressure. This is what the model calculates. Higher available fire flow = better fire protection = lower insurance premiums.

The Force Main Deep Dive

    
A force main is a pressurized island in a gravity ocean — a pipe where gravity has failed and water must be pushed uphill. It is the exact boundary between the SWMM5 and EPANET worlds. Understanding its five operational states is essential for correct modeling.


    


      

        
The Five States of a Force Main

        
A force main cycles through five distinct hydraulic states during pump operation. Only State 3 can be modeled identically in both SWMM5 and EPANET. States 1, 2, 4, and 5 are invisible to EPANET.

        
        

          
          
          
          
          
        

        

          
State 1: Empty (Pump Off)

          

        

      


      

        
Flow Regime Transition at d/D = 1.0

        
SWMM5 switches friction equations at the exact moment the pipe transitions from free-surface to pressurized flow:

Key insight: At d/D = 1.0, SWMM5 forces Manning’s and H-W to give the same friction slope. The equivalent n ensures continuity at the transition. The equivalent n is used only during partially-full conditions (States 2, 4, 5). When the pipe is full (State 3), SWMM5 uses H-W or D-W directly — the equivalent n is never invoked.

Common Force Main Modeling Problems

        

          

            
Problem 1: FM Won’t Stay Full

            
Symptom: d/D oscillates 0.8–1.0 with pump on.
Cause: Downstream node max depth too low, limiting HGL.
Fix: Set downstream node max depth to 50+ ft; add a break node with high max depth upstream of discharge.

          

          

            
Problem 2: High Continuity Error

            
Symptom: Continuity error spikes 5–10% at pump on/off.
Cause: Rapid flow change violates Courant condition.
Fix: Reduce Δt to 1–5 sec, use VARIABLE_STEP 0.75, or add pump ramp-up rules.

          

          

            
Problem 3: Open Channel at High Points

            
Symptom: d/D < 1.0 at FM high points with pump running.
Cause: SWMM5 applies open-channel boundary at internal high-point nodes.
Fix: Set all FM node max depths to 50–100 ft; combine segments to remove unnecessary intermediate nodes.

          

          

            
Problem 4: Flow ≠ Expected OP

            
Symptom: Expected 150 GPM from pump curve, get 120 GPM.
Cause: Unaccounted minor losses, dynamic effects, or downstream surcharge.
Fix: Build same system in EPANET to verify. If EPANET also gives 120 GPM, your hand calc missed something.

Pump Affinity Laws (Variable Speed Pumps)

      
EPANET models variable-speed pumps using affinity laws. The speed ratio N/N₀ shifts the entire pump curve. SWMM5 has no equivalent — pumps are either on or off at a fixed speed.

      

        

          
Flow

          
Q₂ = Q₁ × (N₂/N₁)

          
Flow scales linearly with speed

        

        

          
Head

          
H₂ = H₁ × (N₂/N₁)²

          
Head scales with speed squared

        

        

          
Power

          
P₂ = P₁ × (N₂/N₁)³

          
Power scales with speed cubed!

        

      

      
Why this matters: Reducing pump speed by just 20% (N/N₀ = 0.80) reduces power by 49% (0.8³ = 0.512). This is why variable frequency drives (VFDs) are the single most cost-effective energy savings measure for water utilities, and why EPANET's VFD modeling capability is so valuable.

Network Topology: Trees vs Loops

    
SWMM5 networks are typically dendritic (tree-shaped, flowing to outfalls). EPANET networks are typically heavily looped (multiple paths for redundancy). This fundamental difference is not an engineering preference — it is dictated by physics, function, and the fundamental purpose of each system.

    


      

        

          

            SWMM5
            Dendritic (Tree) Network
          

          
          

            
• Flow always moves downstream toward outfall(s)

            
• Gravity provides free, reliable, unidirectional energy

            
• No redundancy — single path from source to outlet

            
• Loops are physically unnecessary and economically wasteful

            
• Kinematic Wave solver works (unidirectional flow)

          

        

        

          

            EPANET
            Looped (Grid) Network
          

          
          

            
• Flow travels multiple paths from source to customer

            
• Pumps can push water in any direction (omnidirectional)

            
• Redundancy — if one pipe breaks, water reroutes

            
• Loops are physically necessary (pumps fail, pipes break)

            
• Gradient Algorithm solves simultaneous loop equations

        The Core Insight: Every difference between SWMM5 and EPANET — equations, solver design, calibration method, failure mode, design criterion — traces back to one physical fact: Gravity pulls water in one direction. Pressure can push water in any direction. This asymmetry determines everything.
      


      

        

          
Why Gravity Makes Trees

          

            
Gravity is unidirectional, passive, and inexhaustible. Water on a hillside has one option: flow downhill. The engineered collection system mirrors the natural watershed because both solve the same physical problem: move water from high ground to low ground.

            
Rain falls everywhere      →  Rain falls on subcatchments
Sheet flow downhill        →  Overland flow to inlets
Rivulets merge to streams  →  8" laterals merge to 12" collectors
Streams merge to creeks    →  Collectors merge to 24" trunk sewers
Creeks merge to rivers     →  Trunk sewers merge to 48" interceptors
River reaches the ocean    →  Interceptor reaches the outfall
ONE destination            →  ONE destination
MANY sources               →  MANY sources
TREE shape                 →  TREE shape

            
Why true loops are rare in gravity systems: A hydraulic loop requires flow that can travel in either direction. Under normal gravity flow, water won’t flow uphill, making loops uncommon. Exceptions exist (dual drainage, surcharged systems, pumped force mains), but the default topology is tree-shaped. A “parallel relief sewer” is not a true loop; both pipes slope the same direction.

            
Why loops are economically wasteful: (1) No reliability benefit — gravity never fails. (2) Pipe slope consumes precious elevation. (3) Collection systems serve area, not points. (4) The outfall is singular and fixed.

          

        

        

          
Why Pressure Makes Loops

          

            
Pressure is omnidirectional, active, and fallible. Pumps can push water any direction, but they require electricity, have parts that wear out, and can fail. Because sources can fail, the network must loop for reliability.

            
DEAD-END FIRE FLOW:
Source → 8" pipe (2000 ft) → Hydrant
  1,500 GPM through ONE 8" pipe
  Velocity: 9.6 ft/s (dangerously high!)
  Headloss: ~95 ft per 1000 ft at C=100
  Available pressure: probably ZERO — FAILS

LOOPED FIRE FLOW:
Source — 8" pipe — Node A — 8" pipe — Hydrant
                        |                       |
Source — 8" pipe — Node B — 8" pipe ———’
  1,500 GPM split across FOUR 8" pipes
  Each pipe carries ~375 GPM
  Velocity: 2.4 ft/s (comfortable)
  Headloss: ~7 ft per 1000 ft — WORKS

            
Headloss reduction is highly nonlinear: Two parallel paths reduce headloss by ~72%, not 50%. Four paths reduce it by ~93%. This is the H-W exponent (1.85) at work.

            
Economic justification: (1) A boil-water advisory costs far more than a $10K–$50K cross-connection. (2) ISO fire insurance ratings require loops. (3) Dead ends cause stagnant water → chlorine decay → bacteria. (4) PRVs enable pressure zone management.

Proof: Why Gravity Networks Must Be Trees

        

          
For a loop (cycle) to exist, water must flow A→B→C→A. But each step must go downhill: A→B means B is lower than A. B→C means C is lower than B. C→A means A is lower than C. But A is higher than B, which is higher than C — so A cannot be lower than C. Contradiction. Cycles are impossible under gravity-only flow. QED.

          
Try to Build a Loop — Every Attempt Fails:

          
ATTEMPT 1: A(100ft) —Pipe 1— B(95ft) —Pipe 2— A(100ft)
  Pipe 1: slopes A→B (downhill) ✓   Pipe 2: slopes A→B (also downhill)
  Both flow SAME direction. Parallel pipes, NOT a loop.

ATTEMPT 2: A(100ft) —— B(100ft) (equal elevations)
  Both pipes are FLAT. Zero slope. Zero flow. Stagnant.
  Violates minimum slope (2 ft/s self-cleansing).

ATTEMPT 3: A(100ft) — B(90ft) — C(90ft) — A???
  B→C is flat (no flow). C→A goes UPHILL. Impossible.

ATTEMPT 4: The ONLY way — add a PUMP:
  A(100) → B(90) by gravity, B → A by FORCE MAIN + pump.
  But you’ve introduced PRESSURE. The force main is an
  EPANET pipe inside a SWMM5 model. Gravity alone can’t close it.

Dead-End Water Quality Problem

        

          
Treated drinking water is perishable. Chlorine decays over time. At dead ends, water can sit for hours to days:

          
Dead-end chlorine decay:
  C(t) = C&sub0; × e^(-kb × t)
  If C&sub0; = 2.0 mg/L, kb = 0.5/day, water age = 3 days:
  C = 2.0 × e^(-0.5 × 3) = 2.0 × 0.22 = 0.45 mg/L
  Minimum required: 0.2 mg/L — AT RISK of falling below

Dead-end problems: bacterial regrowth, DBP formation,
taste/odor complaints, sediment, temperature increase.

Loop fix: cross-connect the dead end → water flows THROUGH
continuously. Water age: hours instead of days.
Chlorine maintained. No stagnation. No complaints.

          
EPANET’s built-in water age analysis directly quantifies this. Utilities use EPANET to identify dead ends and prioritize cross-connections.

        

      


      

        
When SWMM5 Networks DO Have Loops (Exceptions)

        

          

            
Dual Drainage

            
When pipes surcharge, water escapes to the street and re-enters downstream. The loop forms because of failure, not by design. Requires Dynamic Wave routing.

          

          

            
Parallel Relief Sewer

            
Both pipes slope the same direction — flow is unidirectional through both paths. Technically a loop in graph theory, but not a true hydraulic loop. It’s a capacity fix, not a topology choice.

          

          

            
CSO Regulator

            
Flow goes either to interceptor OR to overflow. Both paths go downhill. In graph theory it’s a tree with a branch (Y-junction), not a cycle.

          

          

            
Interconnected / Tidal Systems

            
Historical interconnections or tidal boundaries create bidirectional flow — but these are human decisions or external forcing, not gravity spontaneously forming loops. Require Dynamic Wave routing.

          

        

        
Every exception involves failure (surcharge), pressure (pumps), external forcing (tides), or human intervention (interconnections). None involves gravity spontaneously creating a loop.

SWMM5 Failure Cascade

          

            
1. Storm exceeds design capacity

            
2. Pipes surcharge (d/D > 1.0)

            
3. HGL rises above ground → manhole flooding

            
4. Street flooding / basement backup

            
5. CSO/SSO → receiving water contamination

1. Demand exceeds supply capacity

            
2. Pressure drops below 40 psi (low-pressure zone)

            
3. Pressure drops below 20 psi (inadequate fire flow)

            
4. Negative pressure → no service

            
5. Contamination intrusion risk (backflow)

          

          
Key insight: Failure radiates outward from the disruption, attenuating as alternate paths compensate. A pipe break affects a localized zone — loops provide hydraulic “short circuits” around it.

Tree Failure: The 5-Phase Upstream Cascade

          
PHASE 1 — Storm intensifies
  Branch A: 8 CFS | B: 5 CFS | C: 11 CFS | D: 4 CFS
  Total: 28 CFS.  Trunk capacity: 20 CFS.
  TRUNK IS OVERWHELMED.

PHASE 2 — Trunk surcharges
  Excess = 28 - 20 = +8 CFS stored in manhole
  As ≈ 16 ft² → dH/dt = 8/16 = 0.5 ft/s
  Head rises 30 ft in one minute!
  (This is why SWMM5 needs tiny timesteps near surcharge.)

PHASE 3 — Backwater propagates upstream
  Trunk junction head rises 85 → 95 ft:
  Branch A slope: (100-85)/500=3% → (100-95)/500=1%
    Flow capacity REDUCED 42%. Branch A backs up.
  Branch C slope: (97-85)/400=3% → (97-95)/400=0.5%
    Flow capacity REDUCED 65%. Branch C backs up rapidly.
  EVERY branch sees reduced gradient simultaneously.

PHASE 4 — Flooding at weakest point
  Branch A, Junct A3: 2 ft freeboard → FLOODS
  Branch C, Junct C5: 1 ft freeboard → FLOODS
  Cause is the trunk 1,000+ ft DOWNSTREAM.

PHASE 5 — Surface flow creates dual drainage loops
  Water from C5 flows along street, re-enters downstream.
  The tree is temporarily BROKEN by failure.

        

        

          
Loop Failure: The 3-Phase Outward Redistribution

          
PHASE 1 — Pipe C breaks (valves isolate it)
  Pipe A: 400 → 500 GPM | Pipe B: 350 → 550 GPM
  Pipe D: 280 → 450 GPM (absorbs Pipe C’s flow)
  Node 1: 60 → 52 psi | Node 2: 55 → 50 psi
  Node 3: 50 → 35 psi | Node 4: 48 → 38 psi
  All pressures still above 20 psi minimum. ✓

PHASE 2 — Fire breaks out near Node 3 (1,500 GPM)
  Pipe A: → 900 GPM | Pipe B: → 950 GPM (high!)
  Node 3: 18 psi — BELOW MINIMUM (20 psi)
  This is the compound failure: break + fire + peak.
  Service degraded but NOT zero.
  Tank drains to help — buffer of ~4 hours.

PHASE 3 — Pressure impact zone mapping
  Adjacent (≤500 ft): 15-20 psi drop (may be inadequate)
  Nearby (500-1500 ft): 5-10 psi drop (adequate)
  Far (>1500 ft): 1-3 psi drop (barely noticeable)
  Impact ATTENUATES with distance — alternate paths
  absorb more flow further from the break.

SWMM5 Failure Analysis Workflow

          
1. IDENTIFY: Run design storms (2-yr, 10-yr, 25-yr, 100-yr)

2. LOCATE: Find nodes where max depth > crown (surcharge)
           or max depth > ground (flooding)

3. DIAGNOSE: For each flooding node:
   a. Trace DOWNSTREAM along the pipe network
   b. Find the FIRST pipe where d/D reaches 1.0
   c. THIS is the bottleneck CAUSING upstream flooding
   d. Note: bottleneck may be 2, 5, or 10 pipes
      downstream of the flooding location!

4. QUANTIFY:
   • Flooding volume (gal or ft³) at each node
   • Flooding duration (hours)
   • Peak flooding rate (CFS)
   • Max HGL vs ground elevation (freeboard)

5. TRACE IMPACT ZONE:
   From bottleneck, trace UPSTREAM through all branches
   Every node with insufficient freeboard is at risk

6. EVALUATE ALTERNATIVES:
   a. Upsize the bottleneck pipe
   b. Add parallel relief sewer (creates a loop!)
   c. Add upstream detention
   d. Implement LID/green infrastructure
   e. Divert flow to alternate outfall

NOTE: This is a common diagnostic heuristic.
Bottlenecks may also be caused by downstream
boundary conditions (tailwater, tidal), control
settings, junction losses, or storage interactions.
Always check these factors as well.

          
Diagnostic heuristic: The flooding location may be in Branch A, but the cause is often an undersized trunk downstream. Always trace downstream to identify potential bottlenecks — but also check boundary conditions, control settings, and tailwater effects.

        

        

          
EPANET Failure Analysis Workflow

          
1. ESTABLISH BASELINE:
   Run normal operations (24-hr or 7-day EPS)
   Verify all pressures above 20 psi minimum
   Record baseline pressures/flows at all nodes

2. PIPE BREAK ANALYSIS (for each critical pipe):
   a. Close the pipe (Status = CLOSED)
   b. Run steady-state or EPS
   c. Compare pressures to minimum (20 psi)
   d. Map the “impact zone” of inadequate pressure
   e. Quantify: deficit (psi), affected customers

3. FIRE FLOW + BREAK (compound scenario):
   a. Close pipe + add 1,500 GPM demand at hydrant
   b. Record pressure at hydrant and neighbors
   c. This is the DESIGN WORST CASE

4. PEAK DEMAND + BREAK + FIRE (nightmare):
   a. At maximum day demand hour
   b. Close one critical main
   c. Add fire flow demand at most vulnerable hydrant
   d. Three simultaneous stresses → design event

5. EVALUATE ALTERNATIVES:
   a. Add cross-connections (increase loop density)
   b. Upsize critical mains
   c. Add emergency interconnections
   d. Install backup generators at pump stations
   e. Add storage tanks in vulnerable zones
   f. Improve valve operations (faster isolation)

          
Key difference: EPANET failure is localized. Pipe breaks near dense loops cause small pressure drops. Breaks near dead ends cause total loss. Connectivity determines severity — the same break has different impacts depending on network structure.

Calibration: Sequential Trees vs Simultaneous Meshes

    
Calibration workflows differ fundamentally due to network topology. SWMM5 calibrates sequentially, branch by branch (upstream to downstream) in a tree. EPANET requires simultaneous whole-network optimization because changing one pipe’s roughness redistributes flow through all loops.

    


      

        

          
SWMM5: Sequential Branch-by-Branch

          
Step 1: Calibrate FURTHEST UPSTREAM subcatchments
        Adjust: imperviousness, width, infiltration
        These are INDEPENDENT of each other

Step 2: Calibrate the COLLECTOR reach
        Upstream inflows now correct
        Adjust: Manning's n, add RDII if needed

Step 3: Calibrate the TRUNK reach
        All branches above are correct
        Errors must be in trunk parameters

Step 4: Verify at the OUTFALL
        If flow matches → model calibrated

          
Errors are localized — wrong flow at Monitor A means problem is in Subcatchment A. Sensitivity is contained within the branch.

        

        

          
EPANET: Simultaneous Optimization

          
PROBLEM: Cannot calibrate Pipe 1 independently
         Changing C in Pipe 1:
         → More flow through Pipe 1
         → Less flow through Pipe 2
         → Pressures change EVERYWHERE
         → Everything is coupled

SOLUTION: Minimize Σ(P_model - P_measured)²
          Adjust: C₁,C₂...Cₙ simultaneously
          Plus: D₁,D₂...Dₘ (demands)
          Subject to: EPANET equations satisfied

          
No sequential approach possible. Changing roughness of one pipe affects pressures several blocks away. Requires simultaneous nonlinear optimization.

EPANET Calibration Strategies (5 Methods)

        

          

            
1. Fire Flow Tests (The Gold Standard)

            

              
Open hydrant, measure flow Q (known). Measure pressure at hydrant and nearby nodes. Since headloss hf = f(C, Q, D, L) and Q, D, L are measured, the only unknown is C.

              
Why this is best: The fire flow (1,000–2,000 GPM) is so large relative to normal demands that it dominates the headloss — uncertainty in background demands doesn’t matter. You’re running a controlled experiment on the real system. Accuracy: C within ±10.

              
A utility that runs 20 fire flow tests across its system can calibrate the major transmission and distribution mains with reasonable confidence.

            

          

          

            
2. C-Factor Grouping

            
Instead of calibrating 5,000 individual pipe roughnesses, group by physical characteristics: Cast iron pre-1950 → C=?, Cast iron 1950–1980 → C=?, Ductile iron post-1980 → C=?, PVC all ages → C=?, Concrete transmission → C=?. Now 5 unknowns instead of 5,000. The grouping reflects actual physics: pipes of the same material and age experience similar corrosion and tuberculation.

          

          

            
3. Tank Level Matching

            
Compare modeled vs measured tank levels over 24–48 hours. Tank level integrates the entire system’s behavior: if modeled level drops faster than measured → demands too high or roughness too high. If slower → demands too low or roughness too low. This is the most informative single measurement for EPANET calibration.

          

          

            
4. District Metered Areas (DMAs)

            
Install flow meters at all entry/exit points of an isolated zone. Net inflow = ΣQin − ΣQout = total demand in DMA. Compare to modeled demand → adjust demand allocation. Bonus: Nighttime minimum flow (2–4 AM) ≈ leakage. This calibrates emitter coefficients for background leakage.

          

          

            
5. Automated Optimization

            
Formulate as: Minimize Σwi·(Pmodel,i − Pmeasured,i)² with decision variables = C-factor per group + demand multiplier per zone. Solve with GA, Levenberg-Marquardt, SCE, or Bayesian methods. EPANET runs in seconds, so thousands of iterations in minutes. This is a huge advantage over SWMM5 calibration where each run takes minutes to hours.

Fire Flow Test: The Gold Standard Worked Example

        
FIRE FLOW TEST PROTOCOL:
1. Select hydrant in area of interest
2. Open hydrant fully → measure flow Q (pitot gauge)
3. Record pressure:
   a. Flowing hydrant (residual pressure)
   b. Nearby hydrant (static before, residual during)
   c. SCADA points if available
4. Close hydrant → record recovery pressure

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  Static pressure:   65 psi (before opening hydrant)
  Residual pressure: 38 psi (while flowing 1,200 GPM)
  Pressure drop:     27 psi = 62 ft of headloss

  Path: 5,000 ft of 12" main from reservoir to hydrant

  Solving Hazen-Williams for C (US-CFS form: Q in ft³/s, d in ft):
  Q = 1,200 GPM ÷ 448.831 = 2.673 ft³/s, d = 12" ÷ 12 = 1.0 ft
  62 = 4.727 × 5,000 × 2.673^1.852 / (C^1.852 × 1.0^4.87)
  → C ≈ 95 (moderately corroded)

WHY THIS IS BEST:
  Fire flow (1,000-2,000 GPM) DOMINATES the headloss.
  Normal demands (~10-50 GPM/node) are noise.
  Demand uncertainty DOESN’T MATTER.
  It’s a controlled experiment on the real system.
  Accuracy: C within ±10.

  20 tests across the system → well-constrained calibration.

      


      

        
Automated Optimization Formulation

        
Minimize:  Σ&sub1; w&sub1; × (P_model,i - P_measured,i)²
         + Σj wj × (T_model,j - T_measured,j)²
         (pressure residuals + tank level residuals)

Decision variables:
  C&sub1;,C&sub2;,...,C&sub6; (roughness per material/age group)
  DM&sub1;,DM&sub2;,...,DM_k (demand multiplier per zone)

Constraints:
  60 ≤ C_i ≤ 150     (physical bounds on roughness)
  0.7 ≤ DM_j ≤ 1.3   (±30% demand uncertainty)
  EPANET equations satisfied at every node

FEASIBILITY:
  Each EPANET run: ~1 second (for 10,000-node network)
  5,000 iterations: ~83 minutes  —  PRACTICAL
  Compare: SWMM5 at minutes/run → 5,000 iterations = DAYS

      


      

        

          
What Makes Tree Calibration Tractable

          

            
Errors are localized. If Monitor E shows too much flow but Monitor F matches, the problem is ONLY in Subcatchments upstream of E.

            
Small search space per step. 5–8 parameters for 1–2 subcatchments at a time, not 50 parameters for 25 subcatchments simultaneously.

            
Sensitivity is local. Increasing imperviousness in Subcatchment A has zero effect on flow at Monitor F. The sensitivity matrix is block-diagonal.

            
Order is clear. Upstream to downstream. Each step benefits from previous steps being correct.

          

        

        

          
What Makes Tree Calibration HARD

          

            
Nonlinear hydrology. Infiltration has threshold behavior — once depression storage fills, runoff begins suddenly. Small parameter changes near thresholds cause large output changes.

            
Equifinality. High imperviousness + high depression storage mimics low imperviousness + low depression storage. Multiple storms needed to break degeneracy.

            
Limited monitoring. 3 monitors for 20 subcatchments. Individual errors may cancel within groups.

            
RDII is the wild card. Rainfall-dependent infiltration/inflow enters through pipe defects. The RTK unit hydrograph has 9 parameters per soil group per gage — the most challenging calibration component.

            
Temporal variation. Antecedent moisture conditions dramatically affect infiltration. Model calibrated to a wet event may fail on a dry event.

PART 2: Bedrock Truths — What Is Actually, Provably True


      
Physics Layer (Non-Negotiable)


      

        Truth 1: Conservation of Mass (Continuity)
        
∂A/∂t + ∂Q/∂x = qlateral

        
    
          
  • In a conduit of cross-section A, flow Q, with possible lateral inflow q.
    • 
          
  • EPANET sets ∂A/∂t = 0 (steady) and q = 0. Both are wrong for drainage.
    • 
          
  • In SWMM's world, ∂A/∂t is the entire game. The pipe fills and drains. Water depth changes continuously. Lateral inflows from subcatchments are the primary driver.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 2: Conservation of Momentum (St. Venant / Shallow Water)
        
∂Q/∂t + ∂(Q²/A)/∂x + gA·∂y/∂x = gA·(S₀ − Sf)

        
    
          
  • ∂Q/∂t = local acceleration (absent in EPANET entirely)
    • 
          
  • ∂(Q²/A)/∂x = convective acceleration (absent in EPANET)
    • 
          
  • gA·∂y/∂x = pressure gradient from depth variation
    • 
          
  • gA·Sf = friction slope (the ONLY term EPANET uses, via H-W or D-W)
    • 
          
  • gA·S₀ = gravity (bed slope — the primary driver in open channels)
    • 
        

        
This is the fundamental equation. EPANET solves a degenerate, zero-inertia, full-pipe special case. SWMM5 dynamic wave solves the complete set.

      


      

        Truth 3: Free Surface Exists Until It Doesn't
        
    
          
  • Water in a gravity drain has a free surface. Depth varies in space and time.
    • 
          
  • Cross-sectional area A = f(y) is nonlinear for circular pipes.
    • 
          
  • When y → D (pipe diameter), the system transitions to pressurized (surcharge).
    • 
          
  • This transition is the single hardest numerical problem in computational hydraulics — the Preissmann slot or similar artifice is required.
    • 
          
  • EPANET never faces this because it starts and stays pressurized. SWMM5 modelers must live at this boundary.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 4: Gravity Is the Engine, Not Pumps
        
    
          
  • In drainage: S₀ (bed slope) provides the energy. Pumps are exceptions, not the rule.
    • 
          
  • In EPANET: pumps and reservoir head provide the energy. Gravity is secondary.
    • 
          
  • This inverts the entire energy mental model.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 5: The Catchment Is the Boundary Condition
        
    
          
  • Rainfall → infiltration → depression storage → overland flow → inlet → pipe
    • 
          
  • This chain is the "reservoir" equivalent. It is nonlinear, hysteretic, spatially distributed, and time-varying.
    • 
          
  • There is no equivalent in EPANET. You cannot skip it. It is not a "nice to have."
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 6: Flow Can Reverse. Routinely.
        
    
          
  • Backwater from a downstream boundary (tailwater, tide, surcharge) propagates upstream.
    • 
          
  • Flow direction in a link can change sign within a single event.
    • 
          
  • EPANET's mental model of "flow goes from high pressure to low pressure in a fixed pattern" breaks completely.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 7: Dry Elements Are Normal
        
    
          
  • Pipes can be empty. Nodes can be dry. Zero depth, zero flow is a valid state.
    • 
          
  • EPANET never deals with this. SWMM5 must handle wetting and drying fronts.
    • 
          
  • This introduces discontinuities that wreck naive solvers.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 8: Time Step Size Has Physical Meaning
        
Courant Number: Cr = (V + c) · Δt / Δx ≤ 1

        
    
          
  • Where V = velocity, c = gravity wave speed √(gA/T), Δt = time step, Δx = conduit length.
    • 
          
  • Violating this causes numerical instability or diffusion (smeared hydrographs).
    • 
          
  • EPANET has no equivalent constraint. An EPANET modeler picking Δt = 5 min "because it worked before" can blow up a SWMM5 dynamic wave model or silently attenuate every peak.
    • 
        

      


      
Mathematical Layer


      

        Truth 9: The System Is a DAE, Not Just Algebraic
        
    
          
  • EPANET solves: F(H, Q) = 0 — nonlinear algebraic equations at each time step.
    • 
          
  • SWMM5 dynamic wave solves: dY/dt = G(Y, Q, t) — coupled ODEs for node depths and conduit flows, with algebraic constraints at boundaries.
    • 
          
  • Newton-Raphson on an algebraic system vs. a time-marching scheme for a hyperbolic PDE system.
    • 
        

      


      

        Truth 10: Manning's n Is Not Hazen-Williams C
        
    
          
  • Manning: Q = (1/n) · A · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2) — open channel and partially full pipe.
    • 
          
  • Hazen-Williams: V = k · C · R^0.63 · S^0.54 — empirical, pressurized pipe only, limited velocity range.
    • 
          
  • The EPANET modeler's muscle memory of "C = 130 for new pipe" is useless. The equivalent instinct is "n = 0.013 for PVC, 0.024 for corrugated metal."
    •

Key Invariants of the Rebuilt Model

      

        
1. The subcatchment is not optional. It is the equivalent of EPANET's reservoir + demand pattern, but it computes inflow from first principles (rainfall, infiltration, routing).

        
2. Nodes have volume. Every junction has a depth, a surface area, and stores water. dH/dt = (ΣQ_in - ΣQ_out) / A_surface. This is the state variable the solver marches forward.

        
3. Links have inertia. dQ/dt ≠ 0. Flow does not instantly adjust. Transient waves propagate. This is why dynamic wave routing exists and why kinematic wave is a lossy approximation (no backwater).

        
4. The flow regime can change within a single conduit. Subcritical → critical → supercritical → hydraulic jump → surcharge. All in one pipe, one time step.

        
5. The system clock is physics, not convenience. Δt is constrained by Courant, not by "how often do I want output." Routing Δt and reporting Δt are decoupled. The SWMM5 default wet-weather Δt of 30 seconds exists for a reason.

        
6. Boundary conditions define the problem. The outfall condition (free discharge, tidal, fixed stage) propagates upstream through the entire system via backwater. An EPANET modeler setting a "free outfall" and expecting no consequences is making a physics choice, not an administrative one.

What Becomes Optional or Obsolete

      

        
1. The "loop equation" mental model. EPANET solves energy loops (Kirchhoff's voltage law analog). SWMM5 dynamic wave doesn't use loop equations at all — it solves node continuity + link momentum as a coupled marching system.

        
2. Demand patterns as a primary concept. There is no demand. There is rainfall, and there is dry weather flow (DWF), which is conceptually similar but mechanically different.

        
3. The idea that negative pressure = model error. In SWMM5, negative depth is the error condition. But "surcharging" (pressure > atmospheric in a pipe) is a normal, expected, physically correct state.

        
4. Pipe sizing by pressure requirement. In drainage, pipe sizing is by: capacity at design flow (Manning's at some target d/D ratio, often 0.8), min/max velocity, minimum slope, downstream invert connectivity. Pressure is not a design variable.

        
5. The assumption that model complexity scales linearly. SWMM5 nonlinearity means adding one surcharging pipe or one undersized inlet can cause cascading instability in the solver.

      


      
Surprising New Capabilities

      

        
1. You can model flooding explicitly. When the system surcharges beyond the ground surface, SWMM5 can pond water above the node, route it back into the system when capacity returns, or lose it as surface flooding.

        
2. You can model storage at any scale. Detention ponds, bioretention cells, green roofs, permeable pavement — all are "storage nodes" or "LID controls" in SWMM5.

        
3. You can model the rainfall-to-outfall chain in a single model. SWMM5 integrates Horton/Green-Ampt/CN infiltration, NRCS/SCS unit hydrograph routing, and St. Venant conduit routing in one executable.

        
4. Kinematic wave as a deliberate simplification. SWMM5 offers KW routing as a choice — dropping the ∂Q/∂t and backwater terms for speed. The SWMM5 modeler can consciously choose their physics fidelity level.

        
5. Real-time control (RTC). SWMM5 can model gates, weirs, pumps, and orifices with control rules that respond to simulated depths, flows, or times. This makes it a dynamic process simulator, not just a design checker.

        
6. LID/GI/SUDS process chains. Low Impact Development controls (green roofs, rain gardens, permeable pavement, rain barrels, bioretention, infiltration trenches, rooftop disconnection, vegetative swales) can be modeled as multi-layer soil column processes within subcatchments.

Everything — the unsteadiness, the free surface, the surcharge transition, the backwater, the reverse flow, the Courant constraint, the solver complexity — follows from the difference between these two mathematical statements.


      
The EPANET equation is a special case (steady, full, no lateral inflow, no inertia, no wave propagation) of the SWMM5 equations. Not the other way around.

    


    
    

      
The transition is not about learning new software buttons. It is about replacing an algebraic, pressurized, demand-driven worldview with a differential, open-channel, rainfall-driven worldview. The physics doesn't care which tool you learned first.

1. Fundamental Equations


      
1.1 Saint-Venant Equations (SWMM5 Dynamic Wave)

      
SWMM5 solves the one-dimensional Saint-Venant equations for unsteady, non-uniform flow:


Continuity:  ∂A/∂t + ∂Q/∂x = q_lat

Momentum (SWMM5 convention):
  ∂Q/∂t + ∂(Q²/A)/∂x + gA·∂y/∂x = gA·(S₀ − Sf) − gA·hL

Equivalent H-based form (H = z₀ + y, so ∂H/∂x = ∂y/∂x − S₀):
  ∂Q/∂t + ∂(Q²/A)/∂x + gA·∂H/∂x + gA·Sf + gA·hL = 0

Where:
  A  = cross-sectional flow area (varies with depth and position)
  Q  = volumetric flow rate
  q_lat = lateral inflow per unit length
  y  = water depth
  S₀ = bed slope = −∂z₀/∂x
  H  = water surface elevation = z₀ + y
  Sf = friction slope (from Manning's equation)
  hL = local (minor) losses
  g  = gravitational acceleration

Momentum equation terms:
  ∂Q/∂t        → Local acceleration
  ∂(Q²/A)/∂x   → Convective acceleration
  gA·∂H/∂x     → Pressure/gravity force
  gA·Sf         → Friction force
  gA·hL         → Local losses


      
1.2 Manning's Equation (SWMM5 Friction)


US Units:  Q = (1.486/n) · A · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2)
SI Units:  Q = (1.0/n) · A · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2)

Friction Slope:
  Sf = [n·Q / (φ·A·R^(2/3))]²    where φ = 1.486 (US) or 1.0 (SI)

Where:
  n = Manning's roughness coefficient
  A = flow area
  R = hydraulic radius = A/P (P = wetted perimeter)
  S = friction slope


      
1.3 Hazen-Williams Equation (EPANET Default)


Headloss (four common forms — same equation, different constants for different unit combos):

  US-CFS:   hf = 4.727 · L · Q^1.852 / (C^1.852 · d^4.87)       Q in ft³/s, d in ft, L in ft, hf in ft
  US-GPM:   hf = 10.44 · L · Q^1.852 / (C^1.852 · d^4.87)        Q in GPM, d in inches, L in ft, hf in ft
  SI:       hf = 10.67 · L · Q^1.852 / (C^1.852 · d^4.87)        Q in m³/s, d in m, L in m, hf in m
  SI-L/s:   hf = 1.131e10 · L · Q^1.852 / (C^1.852 · d^4.87)     Q in L/s, d in mm, L in m, hf in m

  ⚠ This calculator uses the US-CFS form (4.727) internally.
    User inputs (GPM, inches or L/s, mm) are converted to CFS & ft before computation.

Velocity:
  US: V = 1.318 · C · R^0.63 · Sf^0.54
  SI: V = 0.8492 · C · R^0.63 · Sf^0.54

Friction Slope:
  Sf = [V / (Cf · C · R^0.63)]^(1/0.54)    where Cf = 1.318 (US) or 0.8492 (SI)

Where:
  C = Hazen-Williams roughness coefficient (higher = smoother)
  hf = headloss (ft or m)
  L = pipe length
  d = pipe diameter
  R = hydraulic radius (D/4 for full circular pipe)


      
1.4 Darcy-Weisbach Equation (EPANET Alternative)


hf = f · (L/D) · (V²/2g)

Where:
  f = Darcy friction factor (from Moody diagram / Colebrook-White)
  L = pipe length
  D = pipe diameter
  V = flow velocity
  g = gravitational acceleration

Colebrook-White (implicit):
  1/√f = -2·log₁₀(ε/(3.7·D) + 2.51/(Re·√f))

Swamee-Jain (explicit approximation):
  f = 0.25 / [log₁₀(ε/(3.7·D) + 5.74/Re^0.9)]²

    


    
    

      
2. EPANET Solver — Gradient Algorithm


      
2.1 Global Gradient Algorithm (Todini & Pilati 1988, Rossman 2000)

      
The theoretical foundation was laid by Todini and Pilati (1988). EPANET’s implementation uses Rossman’s 2000 reformulation, which restructures the GGA to exploit sparse matrix techniques and the topological structure of pipe networks for computational efficiency.


At each hydraulic timestep, EPANET solves simultaneously:

Node continuity:    ΣQ_in - ΣQ_out = Demand    (at every junction)
Energy equation:    H_i - H_j = hf(Q) + hm(Q)  (along every pipe)

Where:
  H_i, H_j = heads at upstream and downstream nodes
  hf(Q) = friction headloss (Hazen-Williams, Darcy-Weisbach, or Manning)
  hm(Q) = minor loss = K · V²/2g

Solution method:
  1. Linearize nonlinear headloss equations (Newton-Raphson)
  2. Assemble sparse matrix system: [A]{H} = {b}
  3. Solve for node heads H and link flows Q
  4. Check convergence: |ΔQ| < tolerance and |ΔH| < tolerance
  5. Iterate until converged (typically 5-10 iterations)


      
2.2 Extended Period Simulation (EPS)


Tank level update between timesteps:
  V_tank(t+Δt) = V_tank(t) + (ΣQ_in - ΣQ_out) · Δt

Typical timesteps:
  Hydraulic: 1 hour (unconditionally stable)
  Quality:   5 minutes (advection through pipes)
  Pattern:   1 hour (demand multiplier update)

    


    
    

      
3. Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) Condition


Δt ≤ Δx / (V + c)

Where:
  Δt = computational timestep
  Δx = conduit length (spatial step)
  V  = flow velocity
  c  = gravity wave celerity = √(gA/T)
  T  = top width of water surface
  g  = gravitational acceleration (32.2 ft/s² or 9.81 m/s²)

Example: 400 ft pipe, V=5 ft/s, c=15 ft/s → Δt_max = 400/(5+15) = 20 sec
Example: 50 ft pipe, V=10 ft/s, c=20 ft/s → Δt_max = 50/(10+20) = 1.7 sec

    


    
    

      
4. Force Main: Manning's n ↔ Hazen-Williams C


      
4.1 SWMM5 Conversion Formula


Full derivation (setting Manning velocity = H-W velocity):
  n = (φ / (Cf · C)) · R^(2/3 - 0.63) · Sf^(1/2 - 0.54)
  n = (φ / (Cf · C)) · R^(0.0367)     · Sf^(-0.04)
                                          ↑ SWMM drops this term

SWMM5 approximation (forcemain.c):
  n = (φ / Cf) · R^(0.0367) / C

Where:
  φ  = Manning constant: 1.486 (US) or 1.0 (SI)
  Cf = H-W constant: 1.318 (US) or 0.8492 (SI)
  R  = hydraulic radius at full flow = D/4
  C  = Hazen-Williams coefficient

Error from dropped Sf^(-0.04) term:
  At Sf = 0.001: Sf^(-0.04) = 1.32  (32% error in n)
  At Sf = 0.01:  Sf^(-0.04) = 1.10  (10% error in n)
  At Sf = 0.1:   Sf^(-0.04) = 1.04  ( 4% error in n)

5. Water Quality Equations


      
5.1 Chlorine Decay (EPANET)


Bulk decay (first-order):
  C(t) = C₀ · e^(-kb·t)
  kb = bulk decay coefficient (1/day)

Wall decay:
  Rate = kw · (C / (C + kf))    (for kw > 0, first-order wall reaction)
  kw = wall reaction coefficient
  kf = mass transfer coefficient

Combined decay in a pipe segment:
  dC/dt = -kb·C - (2·kw·kf)/(R·(kw + kf)) · C
  R = hydraulic radius


      
5.2 Source Tracing


EPANET: Built-in analysis type — select "Trace" and a source node.
  Returns % of water from that source at every node.

SWMM5 equivalent (DIY):
  Define conservative pollutant: decay = 0
  Set concentration = 100 at source inflow, 0 everywhere else
  Result: concentration at node = % from that source
  Advantage: SWMM supports multiple tracers simultaneously

    


    
    

      
6. Emitter Equation (EPANET)


Q = C_e · P^γ

Where:
  Q   = outflow rate
  C_e = emitter coefficient
  P   = pressure at the node
  γ   = pressure exponent (default 0.5, giving orifice-type flow)

Used for: leaks, sprinklers, fire hydrants, pressure-dependent demands

    


    
    

      
7. Preissmann Slot (SWMM5)


Purpose: Allow Saint-Venant equations to handle pressurized flow
  by maintaining a virtual free surface in surcharged pipes.

Slot width:
  T_slot = g · A_full / c²

Where:
  A_full = full pipe cross-sectional area
  c = target pressure wave celerity (typically ~1000 ft/s)
  g = gravitational acceleration

Example: 48-inch pipe, c=1000 ft/s
  A_full = π(2)²/4 = 12.57 ft²
  T_slot = 32.2 × 12.57 / 1000² = 0.0004 ft (very narrow)

11.2 Flow Regime Transition


d/D < 1.0  →  Manning's equation with equivalent n
d/D = 1.0  →  Transition point (both equations give same Sf)
d/D ≥ 1.0  →  Hazen-Williams directly with C (or D-W with ε)

Key insight: If the pump runs continuously and the force main
is always full, the equivalent n is NEVER USED — SWMM applies
H-W directly. The conversion only matters during pump startup,
shutdown, and cycling when the main drains down and refills.


      
11.3 Force Main System Context


Wet Well (STORAGE) → Pump (PUMP) → Force Main (CONDUIT) → Discharge (JUNCTION)

Total Dynamic Head = Static Lift + h_f(friction) + h_m(minor losses)

Your H-W C choice affects:
  • System head curve intersection with pump curve
  • Pump cycling frequency (less friction → faster drawdown)
  • Pump discharge head (friction loss adds to static lift)

14.4 Accuracy Verification — Hand Calculation Cross-Check

      
This is the single most powerful pedagogical device: the moment when the reader sees, with their own eyes, that the translation actually works. If the numbers don’t match, something is configured wrong.


      

        Verification Problem: Single Pipe, Full Flow, Steady State
        

          Given: 12-inch (1-ft) diameter pipe, 1000 ft long, conveying 500 GPM (1.114 CFS)

          SWMM5: Manning’s n = 0.013 (force main, full-pipe flow)

          EPANET: Hazen-Williams C = 130
        

      




STEP 1: Common geometry
  D = 1.0 ft,  A = π·D²/4 = 0.7854 ft²,  R = D/4 = 0.25 ft
  V = Q/A = 1.114 / 0.7854 = 1.418 ft/s
  Re = V·D/ν = 1.418 × 1.0 / 1.217×10⁻⁵ = 116,500  (turbulent)

STEP 2: Manning headloss (SWMM5)
  Sf = (n·Q / (1.49·A·R^⅔))²
     = (0.013 × 1.114 / (1.49 × 0.7854 × 0.3969))²
     = (0.01448 / 0.4644)² = (0.03118)² = 9.724 × 10⁻⁴ ft/ft
  hf = Sf × L = 9.724×10⁻⁴ × 1000 = 0.972 ft (11.7 in)

STEP 3: Hazen-Williams headloss (EPANET, US-CFS form)
  hf = 4.727 · L · Q^1.852 / (C^1.852 · d^4.87)
     = 4.727 × 1000 × 1.114^1.852 / (130^1.852 × 1.0^4.87)
     = 4.727 × 1000 × 1.2213 / (8222.9 × 1.0)
     = 5773 / 8223 = 0.702 ft (8.4 in)

STEP 4: Compare — they DON'T match!
  Manning (n=0.013):  hf = 0.972 ft
  H-W    (C=130):     hf = 0.702 ft
  Difference: 38.5%  ✗

  This is NOT a bug. It demonstrates that n=0.013 and C=130 are NOT
  equivalent roughness values at this diameter. The n ↔ C mapping is
  diameter-dependent.

STEP 5: Use Darcy-Weisbach as the gold standard
  ε = 0.00085 ft (0.26 mm, new cast iron)
  Colebrook-White:  f = 0.0213
  hf = f·(L/D)·V²/(2g) = 0.0213 × 1000 × 0.03127 = 0.667 ft (8.0 in)

  The D-W equivalents that reproduce this headloss are:
    C ≈ 134  (not 130)     ← use in EPANET
    n ≈ 0.0108 (not 0.013) ← use in SWMM5

STEP 6: Verify the correct equivalents
  Manning (n=0.0108): hf = 0.672 ft  ← matches D-W to 0.7%  ✓
  H-W    (C=134):     hf = 0.658 ft  ← matches D-W to 1.3%  ✓



      

        The lesson: Textbook equivalences like "n=0.013 ≈ C=130" are approximations that work well for some diameters (24″: 0.5% error) and poorly for others (12″: 38% error). For exact agreement between SWMM5 and EPANET, use Darcy-Weisbach with the same ε (roughness height) in both models. D-W is the only equation with a theoretical basis, and both programs support it. When both models use the same equation with the same inputs, they produce identical results for full-pipe steady flow. Any remaining discrepancy means a configuration error — check diameter units (in vs ft), roughness type (n vs C vs ε), flow units (CFS vs GPM), and elevation convention.

16. Historical Context & EPANET Origins


      
16.1 A Brief History of Both Tools

      
SWMM (1971): The Storm Water Management Model was created at the U.S. EPA in cooperation with Metcalf & Eddy, Inc. and the University of Florida. The original development team included Wayne Huber and others. SWMM was one of the first computer models for urban drainage, designed to simulate rainfall-runoff processes and route flow through sewer networks. It was placed in the public domain from its inception, enabling decades of academic research and commercial extension. SWMM5, the current version, was released in 2004 as a complete rewrite in C by Lewis Rossman and others at EPA.

      
EPANET (1993): Lewis Rossman at EPA’s Water Supply and Water Resources Division in Cincinnati developed EPANET as a research tool to understand hydraulic and water-quality behavior in drinking water distribution systems. Like SWMM, it was placed in the public domain. EPANET 2.0 (2000) introduced the solver described throughout this document.

      
Why public domain matters: Both tools were released as public domain software — not merely “open source” with a license, but truly unrestricted. This decision, unusual for its era, enabled the global ecosystem of commercial products (WaterGEMS, InfoWater, PCSWMM, XPSWMM, and many others) that embed these engines. Understanding this shared origin helps explain why SWMM and EPANET share design philosophies (section-based .INP files, IF-THEN-ELSE controls, command-line engines with separate GUIs) despite solving fundamentally different physics.


      
16.2 Dr. Lewis A. Rossman

      
Dr. Rossman authored both EPANET and the current SWMM5 engine — a fact that underscores the deep connection between these tools. He is recognized as EPANET’s original creator.

      
His contribution was not merely the writing of EPANET. It was three deliberate decisions that shaped the field:

      
    
        
  • Public domain release — placing the code in the public domain rather than licensing it, enabling unrestricted academic and commercial use worldwide.
    • 
        
  • Thorough documentation — the EPANET 2 Users Manual remains a model of clarity, with worked examples that allow practitioners to verify their understanding against hand calculations.
    • 
        
  • Modular architecture — building the codebase in a well-organized, readable structure that others could understand, modify, and recompile, creating the foundation for subsequent community development.
    • 
      

      
This combination — open code, clear documentation, extensible architecture — is a model of scientific software development that deserves explicit acknowledgment.


      
16.3 Transition to Community Open Source

      
After more than 30 years of service at EPA, Lew announced his retirement effective April 1, 2014, which the EPANET user community marked as the end of an era for EPA-led development.

      
In 2015, the WDSA Standing Committee of ASCE's EWRI initiated an open-source EPANET project, with development coordinated through the Open Water Analytics (OWA) community, enabling volunteers worldwide to maintain and extend the engine and toolkit.

      
EPA's later release of EPANET 2.2.0 explicitly recognized this new model as a collaboration between EPA and the community developers organized via OWA.


      
16.4 An Eyewitness Account — The Workshop That Started It All

      

        
By the late 1980s, the water distribution modeling world had made impressive strides in hydraulic simulation — steady-state solvers, extended-period simulation, calibration methods — but water quality modeling in pipe networks was still in its infancy. A handful of researchers were working on the problem, but there was no consensus tool, no standard approach, and no coordination between the groups.

        
That changed on February 4–5, 1991, when the AWWA Research Foundation and the U.S. EPA co-sponsored a workshop titled “Water Quality Modeling in Distribution Systems” at EPA’s Andrew W. Breidenbach Environmental Research Center in Cincinnati. Forty-three of the top water distribution modelers in the world were in that room — Uri Shamir, Walter Grayman, Don Wood, Robert Clark, and many others. The purpose was to assess the state of the art, identify gaps, and chart a path forward for water quality modeling in drinking water networks.

        
I was one of the 43 participants. What I remember most vividly is someone who was not on the official program. Lew Rossman, an EPA researcher known at the time primarily for his work on rainfall-runoff modeling (what would eventually become SWMM5), sat quietly through both days of presentations and discussions. He took notes. He asked a few questions. He did not present a paper or give a talk.

        
Then, shortly after the workshop ended, Lew walked up to Bob Clark — who headed EPA’s drinking water research program — and essentially said: “I can build that model.” Bob gave him the green light, and within roughly two years Lew had produced EPANET — a hydraulic and water quality model for distribution systems that was more capable, better documented, and more accessible than anything the 43 experts in that room had envisioned.

        
What made EPANET remarkable was not just the technical achievement but the decisions around it: public domain release, clear documentation with worked examples, and a modular codebase others could read and extend. Those decisions created the entire commercial and academic ecosystem we know today — WaterGEMS, InfoWater, WaterCAD, and dozens of other tools all trace their lineage back to that moment.

        
Years later, Lew turned his attention back to the stormwater side and undertook a complete rewrite of SWMM — producing SWMM5 (released in 2004), which replaced the aging Fortran codebase with clean, modular C. The fact that one person created the definitive models for both drinking water distribution and urban stormwater drainage is, as far as I know, without parallel in the water engineering world.

        
More than thirty years later, the tools Lew built in that Cincinnati office remain the engines beneath virtually every water distribution and urban drainage model in use worldwide. It all started with a quiet man sitting in the back of a workshop, listening carefully, and then volunteering to build what everyone else was only talking about.

        
— Tom Walski, Ph.D., P.E.
Industry Advisor, Bentley Systems
